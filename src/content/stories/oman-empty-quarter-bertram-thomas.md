In October 1930 an Englishman named Bertram Thomas boarded an oil tanker in Muscat and sailed south to Dhofar without telling the British officials who were supposed to know where he was. Thomas was the finance minister and wazir to the Sultan of Muscat and Oman. He was also, by then, quietly obsessed with the Rub' al Khali, the Empty Quarter, the great sand sea of southern Arabia that no European had ever crossed.

He was an unlikely candidate. Thomas was born in 1892 in Pill, a village near Bristol, and worked for the Post Office before the First World War sent him to Belgium and then Mesopotamia. He stayed on in the Arab world as a political officer, rose to run the Sultan's finances, and, as the Royal Scottish Geographical Society tells it, the Sultan once teased him for being unmarried and promised instead to help him to the thing nearest his heart: the Rub' al Khali.

On December 10, 1930, he walked north out of Salalah with Bedouin guides of the Rashid tribe, and in early February 1931 he came out of the sand at Doha, on the Gulf. On the way, his guides showed him a set of tracks and told him they led to a lost city. Sixty years later, those tracks helped send an expedition to a ruin in the Dhofar desert that you can visit today.

The honest answer for travelers is that Thomas's full route, about 1,300 kilometres through what is now Saudi Arabia, is an expedition, not a holiday. The Omani end of it is a different matter: a beach town, a frankincense coast, a disputed lost city and the first of the great dunes, all within a day's drive of each other. Here is what Thomas did, what is there now, and where I would put you.

## How did Thomas actually get across?

On camels, in secret, and with the right guide. Thomas knew he would never get official permission, so he did not ask, and, as Aramco World put it, he went without so much as a by-your-leave to Ibn Sa'ud. He had spent months in Dhofar beforehand, measuring, collecting and making friends with the tribes, and the man who made the crossing possible was his guide, Shaikh Salih bin Kalut al Rashidi al Kathiri. According to the Qatar Digital Library, a tribal feud held bin Kalut up and he reached Thomas only at the last moment.

In the Royal Scottish Geographical Society's account, they struck north over the Qara mountains, through the frankincense groves, and out into the sand. According to the Qatar Digital Library, he carried a prismatic compass, a hydrometer and a sextant, and the map he made of southeast Arabia became a standard reference. The danger, as the Spectator reported in May 1931, was less thirst than the tribes: he crossed contested country under a strong escort, and he moved before word of his presence could spread far ahead of him.

The crossing took about eight weeks. When he reached Doha it made the front page of The New York Times, and in 1932 the Royal Scottish Geographical Society gave him its Livingstone Medal. His account of the journey, Arabia Felix, came out the same year.

## Who was he racing?

Harry St John Philby, and Philby lost. Philby, the British adviser to Ibn Sa'ud and father of the future Soviet spy Kim Philby, had hoped to be first, and could not go without the king's blessing. Both Aramco World and The National report that he shut himself indoors for a week. He made his own crossing in 1932, by a line further west, carrying the Royal Geographical Society's journal with Thomas's map in it.

I like Thomas more for how little he made of it. Those who have written about him describe him as self-effacing and quietly observant, and his gentle manner is what won over desert men who had every reason to distrust a foreign official. The Qatar Digital Library notes that the British Political Resident in the Gulf complained he had neglected his duties, and that a knighthood never came.

## What was the road to Ubar?

A set of tracks in the sand that his guides said led to a lost city, and one of the better stories in Arabian archaeology. As Thomas came toward the sands, his Bedouin companions pointed out a worn caravan route and called it the road to Ubar, a city rich in treasure that God had buried beneath the sands for the wickedness of its people. Thomas marked the track on his map. He never went back to follow it.

Decades later Nicholas Clapp read Thomas and went looking. He brought in NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, whose satellite images, and later radar from the Space Shuttle, picked out faint lines of ancient caravan routes converging on one place: Shisr, a small oasis settlement on the edge of the sands north of Salalah. Excavations led by the archaeologist Juris Zarins uncovered a fortress there, and in February 1992 The New York Times reported that the team was virtually sure it had found Ubar.

Whether it is Ubar is still argued over, and I think that is part of the appeal. Zarins himself later suggested Ubar was a region and a people rather than a single town, and other scholars doubt the identification altogether. What is not in doubt is the site: a fortified caravan stop on the frankincense route, part of which collapsed into a sinkhole as the water beneath it fell away. Since 2000 Shisr has been one of the four parts of the Land of Frankincense World Heritage Site, with the frankincense trees of Wadi Dawkah, the ancient port of Khor Rori and the ruins of Al Baleed on the coast.

## When should you go?

November to early March. Oman's winter is mild and dry, with warm days and cool desert nights, and it is the only sensible time to be in the dunes. Summer in the lowlands runs 35 to 45°C. The exception is the Salalah coast itself, where the khareef monsoon brings mist, light rain and an astonishing green from mid-June to September. It is a beautiful time to see Dhofar's mountains and the wrong time for this trip.

My rule: three nights on the coast, then one or two in the sand, then back to the sea. Thomas did it the other way round, but he had eight weeks.

## How do you see Thomas's desert now?

From Salalah, in a four-wheel drive, with a guide, over one long and very good day. The road climbs north over the same Qara mountains Thomas crossed, drops into the stony wadi at Wadi Dawkah where frankincense trees grow wild, and runs on to Shisr, where you can stand on the walls above the sinkhole and look out at the tracks that started all this. By late afternoon you are in the dunes of the Empty Quarter proper.

That is also where I would want you to spend the night. Thomas described watching the reflection of Venus skip from pool to pool in the desert; you will not find pools, but the sky is the same, and the silence in the sand after dark is the thing most people remember. Private tours from Salalah run this route between October and April, and our advisors can build it around your dates.

## Where should you stay?

Two places in Oman, and one alternative across the border if what you want is the dunes with a spa attached.

On the coast, **Al Baleed Resort Salalah by Anantara** is the obvious base and, for this story, the right one. It has 136 rooms and villas, 88 of them pool villas with their own temperature-controlled pools, on a palm-lined stretch of beach between the Arabian Sea and a freshwater lagoon, and it sits right beside the ruins of Al Baleed and the Museum of the Land of Frankincense. It is in the MICHELIN Guide's hotel selection, though it does not hold a Key. Ask for a pool villa, and let our advisors quote current rates.

In the sand, **Souly Lodge** at Ramlat Hashmann, about four hours north of Salalah, is the only fixed camp on the Omani edge of the Empty Quarter: four air-conditioned bungalows and ten tents, each with its own bathroom, dinner under the stars and a pool fed by sulphur water. It is simple and it serves no alcohol. Book a bungalow. You are not here for the thread count; you are here to wake up among red dunes that look exactly as they did in 1930.

If you would rather see the Empty Quarter in comfort, **Qasr Al Sarab Desert Resort by Anantara** sits on its edge in Abu Dhabi's Liwa Desert, about 90 minutes from the city, with 140 rooms, 14 suites and 53 pool villas, camel treks into the dunes and one MICHELIN Key. Thomas never came this way, but it is the same sand sea, and for a first night in the desert it is the softest landing in Arabia.

## The crossing, 85 years on

In December 2015 the British explorer Mark Evans set out from Salalah to retrace Thomas's route to Doha. With him was Sheikh Mubarak bin Kalut, the great-great-grandson of the guide who had taken Thomas across. They had GPS, smartphones and vehicles carrying their water. It still took them about fifty days.

That is the part of this story that stays with me. The desert has not got smaller, and the families who knew it then still know it now. Thomas died in 1950, and his name is not well known. But the tracks he wrote down are still out there north of Salalah, and so, on a cool January evening, is the view he had.
