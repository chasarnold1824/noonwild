On July 11, 1897, in a small, sheltered bay at the northwest corner of Spitsbergen, three Swedes climbed into the basket of a hydrogen balloon and cast off for the North Pole. Salomon August Andrée was a Swedish engineer from the town of Gränna. Nils Strindberg was the photographer. Knut Frænkel had joined only that year. The balloon was called Örnen, the Eagle, and the plan was to let the summer wind carry it across the top of the world.

Within minutes most of the drag ropes they meant to steer by had slipped away. The balloon rose higher than intended, drifted off to the northeast, and was not seen again. Then, for thirty-three years, nothing. In August 1930 a Norwegian vessel working off Kvitøya, a small, ice-capped island at the far eastern edge of Svalbard, came across their last camp. Among what was recovered was Strindberg's film. It was developed in Stockholm that year, and the pictures came out.

I find this one of the strangest and most moving stories in polar travel, partly because it has two places attached to it, both of which you can still reach by ship: the bay where they left and the island where they ended. The honest answer is that one is a permit and a good landing, and the other is a matter of ice and luck. Here is what happened, what is there now, and how I would plan it.

## What was Andrée actually trying to do?

Fly to the North Pole when everyone else was trying to walk, sledge or drift there. Andrée's idea was that a large hydrogen balloon, trailing heavy ropes along the ice to slow it and give it a little steering, could ride the wind across the polar basin in days rather than months. Alfred Nobel was among his backers.

He tried first in 1896. The expedition sailed to Danskøya on the steamer Virgo, built a balloon shed, a house and a gas works on the shore, and waited for a south wind that never came. The bay, Virgohamna, takes its name from that ship, according to the Norwegian Polar Institute. During that summer's postponement the meteorologist Nils Ekholm left the project, unconvinced the balloon would hold its gas, and Frænkel took his place for the second attempt.

## What happened after the balloon took off?

It came down on the ice three days later, and the walk home took the rest of their lives. The loss of the drag ropes at the start meant the balloon flew higher and less controllably than designed, and ice forming on the envelope weighed it down. After about 65 hours aloft, on July 14, 1897, Örnen settled on the pack ice at almost 83 degrees north. One of the carrier pigeons they released two days into the flight was later caught, carrying a note that all was well on board. It was the last word anyone had from them for a generation.

What followed is the part people underestimate. With sledges, a canvas boat and the supplies they could haul, the three men set out across moving ice toward Franz Josef Land. When the drift defeated them, they built a camp on a floe in September and let the ice carry them. Strindberg kept photographing. On October 5, 1897, the ice brought them ashore on Kvitøya, and within weeks all three were dead. Strindberg died first, and the other two buried him in a crevice in the rocks.

How they died is still argued over. Trichinosis from polar bear meat, carbon monoxide from their stove and, in the Swedish doctor Bea Uusma's more recent reading of the evidence, a polar bear attack have all been proposed. The Grenna Museum, which holds the expedition's collection, still lists the cause as unknown, which I think is the right place to leave it.

## How were they found, and what were the photographs?

By chance, in August 1930, by a Norwegian vessel called the Bratvaag, on a scientific expedition led by Gunnar Horn, whose crew were also hunting walrus around the island. They found the camp at the southwest point of Kvitøya, now called Andréeneset, with a half-buried boat, equipment and the remains of Andrée and Strindberg. About a month later a second ship, the Isbjørn, chartered by journalists, found more, including Frænkel.

The film is the reason the story still lands. Strindberg's exposed rolls had lain in the ice for 33 years. In 1930 John Hertzberg, at the photography department of the Royal Institute of Technology in Stockholm, developed them, and the images that survived show the balloon on the ice, the camp, the sledges, the men at work. They are blurred, scratched and extraordinary. The remains were brought home to Stockholm on October 5, 1930, thirty-three years to the day after the three had reached Kvitøya, and were cremated.

## What is left at Virgohamna, and can you land there?

More than you would expect, and yes, with permission. Virgohamna is one of the most important cultural heritage sites in Svalbard, and it holds three layers of history in one small bay. The oldest are the foundations of Dutch blubber ovens and whalers' graves from a station set up in 1636. Then come Andrée's traces: nails, bolts, wire, timber and piles of rusting iron filings from the gas works that made the hydrogen. Beside them lies the wreckage of the American journalist Walter Wellman's airship base, from his failed attempts on the Pole in 1906, 1907 and 1909, including the collapsed hangar and gas tanks from his airship, America.

The rules are strict, and I think rightly so. Anyone going ashore needs written permission from the Governor of Svalbard, visitors follow a set route and must not step on the remains, and the island's terrain is boulder and scree rather than tundra. Harbour seals often haul out near the water. This is a landing for people who like to stand where something happened, and it is the only place where you can see what the start of a polar flight in 1897 actually required.

## Can you get to Kvitøya?

Sometimes, and that is the honest answer. Kvitøya is almost entirely covered by its ice cap, with only a few small, barren patches of rock showing, and for much of the year it is surrounded by pack ice. It is usually reachable late in the summer, but expedition operators are careful to say it is not always accessible. At Andréeneset a simple memorial stands near the remains of the last camp. Polar bears and walrus are common on the island, which makes the landing itself a judgment call for the expedition leader on the day.

My rule: book a voyage that names Kvitøya in its itinerary, sail in July or August, and treat reaching it as a bonus rather than the point. If you need the island to be guaranteed, this is the wrong trip.

## When should you go, and how big should the ship be?

July or August, on a ship of under 200 passengers. The expedition season opens as the sea ice retreats in late May and June, and August is when full circumnavigations and the remote north and east, Kvitøya included, become realistic. Svalbard has continuous daylight from late April to late August, which means long landings and a ship that can keep moving through the night.

Ship size matters more here than almost anywhere. Since January 1, 2025, ships calling in Svalbard's protected areas may carry no more than 200 passengers, landings are limited to designated sites with a qualified guide, and you must stay at least 300 metres from a polar bear, 500 metres from March to June. That leaves the far north to the small expedition ships, which is where you want to be anyway.

Getting there is simple. SAS and Norwegian fly to Longyearbyen from Oslo in about three hours and from Tromsø in about an hour and forty minutes, and most expedition voyages begin and end there.

## The other end of the story

If the expedition gets hold of you, the last stop is not in the Arctic at all. The expedition's collection is held at the Grenna Museum's Polar Center in Gränna, the town in Sweden where he was born. It is the quiet coda to a trip that begins in Longyearbyen.

What stays with me is not the failure. Everyone knew in 1897 that the balloon was a gamble. It is that the three of them kept writing and photographing all the way to the end, as if somebody would one day come along and read it. Somebody did. You can stand in the bay where they left, and on a good August day, on the island where they stopped.
