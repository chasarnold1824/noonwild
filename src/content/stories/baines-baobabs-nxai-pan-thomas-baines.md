On Wednesday the 21st of May 1862, a forty-one-year-old painter walked away from his wagons on the edge of a salt pan in what is now northern Botswana, looking for something to shoot for the pot. He came back with nothing in his pouch, but on the way he passed a group of baobabs he had seen from the wagon the day before, and he stopped. "Five full-sized trees and two or three younger ones were standing," he wrote, "so that when in leaf their foliage must form one magnificent shade. One gigantic trunk had fallen and lay prostrate, but still losing none of its vitality, sent forth branches and young leaves like the rest." Then he sat down and painted them.

The painter was Thomas Baines, and three years earlier David Livingstone had thrown him off the most famous expedition in Africa. The trees are Baines' Baobabs, and they are still there, on the rim of Kudiakam Pan in Nxai Pan National Park. Stand where he stood and the group looks, by every account I have read, almost exactly as it does in his watercolour: the same leaning trunks, the same fallen giant still growing along the ground. There are not many places on earth where you can hold up a painting from 1862 and see so little difference.

The honest answer on visiting them is that the trees are the easy part. They sit about 30 kilometres inside a park with one gate and sand tracks, on a pan that turns to mud when it rains, and most of the people who reach them do it as a long day out from a camp. Here is Baines's story, and how I would follow it now.

## Who was Thomas Baines?

A coach painter's apprentice who ended up on three expeditions. Baines was born in King's Lynn, Norfolk, in 1820, apprenticed as a teenager to a coach painter, and sailed for the Cape in 1842. There he made his name as a painter, and during the Eighth Frontier War, from 1850 to 1853, as an official war artist.

What he became was something rarer: an artist who could also be trusted with the stores. In 1855 he went to northern Australia as artist and storekeeper on Augustus Gregory's North Australian Expedition, which named a Mount Baines and a Baines River after him. In 1858 that record took him onto Livingstone's Zambezi Expedition in the same double role. It went badly. Livingstone's brother Charles accused him of using expedition canvas for paintings he gave to Portuguese officials, of wasting stores and time, and in 1859 Livingstone dismissed him. He was never given a public chance to answer, and most historians now treat the dismissal as unfair. Baines plainly did not forget it. In the same passage where he describes the baobabs, he cannot resist noting the "metallic coppery-looking red and yellow which Dr. Livingstone was wont so strenuously to object to in my pictures."

## What happened on the road to the Victoria Falls?

He went back to Africa and got there anyway, the long way round. In 1861 Baines joined James Chapman, a trader, hunter and photographer, on a wagon journey that began at Walvis Bay on the Atlantic coast of what is now Namibia, crossed the Kalahari by way of Lake Ngami, and was aimed squarely at the falls Livingstone had made famous. It took them well over a year. By May 1862 they were skirting the great salt pans of the Makgadikgadi, which is where the baobabs come into the story, and on 23 July 1862 Baines wrote the line he had been waiting three years to write: the next day's walk was "the last march between us and the falls."

The two men made an odd and productive pair. Chapman carried a camera, unusual enough in the interior in 1862, and Baines recorded with professional admiration that Chapman "made some of the most beautiful stereoscopic pictures I have ever seen, including the whole group of eager Bushmen, and in one picture myself in the act of sketching." Baines, meanwhile, drew everything: game, people, wagons, landscapes, and at the falls a long series of views. He published the journey as *Explorations in South-West Africa* in 1864 and a folio of lithographs, *The Victoria Falls, Zambesi River, sketched on the spot*, in 1865. Chapman's own account, *Travels in the Interior of South Africa*, followed in 1868.

## What did Baines actually paint?

A sketchbook page, not a grand canvas, which is part of its charm. The sheet that survives has a brindled gnu, a wildebeest, studied at the top, and the baobabs below, under a title that calls them "a group of Baobabs on the north west spruit of Mtwetwe Salt Pan", his spelling of Ntwetwe, the great pan to the south-east. Baines signed and dated his sketches as he went, and the caption reads "Wednesday 21 May pm 1862, T. Baines." It is the afternoon light of a Kalahari May, the trees bare or nearly so, the ground pale around them.

What makes it worth a journey is the comparison. Baobabs grow so slowly, and these have been so little disturbed, that the group in his picture is recognisably the group on the pan today. Lonely Planet notes that a single branch is the only loss; the self-drive guides at Tracks4Africa call them seemingly unchanged. The clump is usually described as seven trees, which matches Baines's count of five full-sized and two or three younger ones closely enough, and the fallen trunk he described is still there, still alive, still putting out branches along the ground.

## What is Chapman's Baobab, and can you still see it?

No, and it is worth knowing why before you go. The other great tree of this stretch, Chapman's Baobab near Gweta on the edge of Ntwetwe Pan, named for Baines's travelling companion, collapsed on 7 January 2016. For generations of travellers before it fell it was said to have served as a post office, with letters left in its trunk for the next wagon party to carry on. Some guides still stop at the fallen tree. It is a reminder that baobabs are not immortal, and that the group at Kudiakam, still standing after 164 years of being looked at, is something of a survivor.

## Where are the baobabs, and how do you get there?

In Nxai Pan National Park, a reserve of about 2,578 square kilometres that was declared in 1970 and extended in 1992 to take in Kudiakam Pan and the baobabs. The park has one gate, on the A3 between Nata and Maun about 65 kilometres west of Gweta, and the baobabs are about 30 kilometres from it on a sand track that needs a 4x4; allow an hour from the gate. Maun is roughly three hours' drive away on tar.

International travelers usually arrive through Maun, which Airlink serves nonstop from Johannesburg and Cape Town. From there a light aircraft to the Nxai Pan airstrip takes about 40 minutes, and the camp collects you. You can self-drive, and serious overlanders do, but I would not make that your first time in the Kalahari: the sand is deep, the pans are unforgiving after rain, and the guides who drive them every week know which tracks are holding.

Park entry for international visitors is about 380 pula per adult per day. The government planned to raise it to 500 pula in April 2026 and postponed the increase, with no new date as I write, so check the figure when you book. There are three unfenced campsites near the trees, each for up to 12 people, with a pit toilet, a bucket shower and no drinking water, booked in advance through the concession holder in Maun. You may not camp under the baobabs themselves, which is as it should be.

## When should you go?

May to October for the baobabs, December to March for the zebra. That is the rule, and the two halves of the year are different trips.

The dry season, from May, is Baines's season. The tracks are firm, the drive to Kudiakam is predictable, and in May the trees are as he saw them, leaves thinning, the pan pale and wide. Game is sparser, and by September and October it is hot and dusty, but the light at the baobabs late in the day is the reason you came.

The rains, from about November or December to March and sometimes April, turn Nxai Pan green and bring the zebra. Thousands of them move onto the grass with the first storms, and Kwando calls it the second-largest zebra migration in Africa. It is the better wildlife trip. It is also the season when pan tracks can turn to mud that stops vehicles, so the baobab day becomes a matter of luck and your guide's judgment. If the trees are the reason for the journey, go dry. If you want both, go in late March or April and accept that one of them may not cooperate.

## Where should you stay?

Inside the park, there is really one choice. **Nxai Pan Camp**, run by Kwando Safaris, is the only permanent camp in Nxai Pan National Park: eight thatched suites and one family suite, all facing a waterhole where elephants come to drink, a pool beside the main area, and solar power. The activity that matters here is the full-day picnic trip to Baines' Baobabs on Kudiakam Pan, which one guest on Kwando's own page calls well worth the long day. In the rains, the zebra come to you. Three nights is right, so that the baobab day can move if the weather does. Let our advisors quote current rates.

For the second half of the trip, go south to the Makgadikgadi proper, the country Baines was skirting when he found the trees. **Jack's Camp**, Natural Selection's camp on the edge of the pans, reopened in 2021 after a complete rebuild and has nine tents of 270 square metres each, every one with its own plunge pool, plus a pool pavilion and a Persian tea tent that feels like a Victorian traveller's idea of the desert. It is open all year. The draw is the pans themselves: habituated meerkats that use your guide as a lookout post, walks with Zu/'hoasi guides, and from April to October quad bikes out onto the salt, where there is nothing between you and the horizon. Jack's does not list a Baines trip, but its guests can book a two- or three-night quad-bike fly camp to Kubu Island and its own baobabs, for those 12 and over.

Three nights at Nxai Pan and three at Jack's, linked by a short flight, is the trip I would build, ideally with the Okavango Delta before or after, since you will fly through Maun anyway.

## What happened to Baines?

He never really stopped travelling. In 1868 and 1869 he led a gold prospecting venture into Matabeleland and Mashonaland, and he spent his last years trying to make it pay. He died in Durban on 8 May 1875, of dysentery, before the venture made him anything. Livingstone, who had sacked him, is remembered everywhere. Baines is remembered, for most travelers, by a clump of trees.

I think he would have taken that. He was a man who went to Africa to draw what was in front of him, and he drew those baobabs so faithfully that you can still check his work. Sit on the pan in the late afternoon, with the shadows of the trunks stretching out across the salt, and you are looking at the same thing he was, on a Wednesday in May, a long way from anywhere, with nothing in his pouch.
