On 14 July 1902, nine years before the expedition that made Machu Picchu famous, a tenant farmer from the Cusco region named Agustín Lizárraga climbed the ridge above the Urubamba with two companions, Enrique Palma and Gabino Sánchez, and walked into a city of overgrown granite. Before he left he did what people have done in ruins for as long as there have been ruins. He took a piece of charcoal and wrote his name and the date on the wall of the Temple of the Three Windows.

On 24 July 1911 Hiram Bingham, a Yale lecturer looking for the last capital of the Incas, came up the same slope behind a local farmer, Melchor Arteaga, a police sergeant named Carrasco and, for the final stretch, a small boy from one of the families farming the terraces. He found the city, and he found the name on the wall. In his notebook he wrote what Arteaga had told him: "Agustín Lizárraga is discoverer of Machu Picchu and lives at San Miguel Bridge just before passing." Then he went home and, over the following decades, told the story with himself as its discoverer.

The honest answer to "who found Machu Picchu" is that the people farming its terraces never lost it. But Lizárraga is the outsider with the best claim, and he is the reason I like this trip more than its reputation. Machu Picchu is the most famous ruin in South America, it is crowded, its ticket system has changed repeatedly since 2024, and the hotels around it range from extraordinary to merely convenient. Here is how it works now, when to go, and the seven places I would put you.

## Who was Agustín Lizárraga?

A farmer and mule driver who knew the valley better than any of the foreigners who came through it. He rented land in the area, worked the Cusco road with his mules and lived near the San Miguel bridge below the ridge, which is why Bingham's guides mentioned him by name. He was away when Bingham came looking for him in 1911.

What Lizárraga left behind was the inscription, "A. Lizárraga 1902" by most accounts, in charcoal on the Temple of the Three Windows, the building whose three trapezoid openings frame the mountains. Bingham photographed it. By most accounts he then had the charcoal cleaned off, which is why you will not see it today, and why for most of the century his own version of events went unchallenged.

The ending is a sad one. Lizárraga drowned in the Vilcanota, the upper Urubamba, in 1912, the year after Bingham's visit, and his body was never recovered. Peruvian historians and the press kept his name alive, and Cusco has since honoured him, but he is still a footnote in most of the books sold at the gate.

Two notes of honesty about the record. Some sources date his climb to 1901 rather than 1902, and he may not have been the first outsider either: a German engineer, Augusto Berns, has been proposed as a visitor in the late 1860s, and a map drawn in 1874 names the peaks of Machu Picchu, though it shows no ruins. What nobody disputes is that the name was on the wall when Bingham arrived.

## What changed when Bingham arrived?

Everything, and quickly. Bingham came back in 1912 with Yale and the National Geographic Society, cleared the site and photographed it, and those photographs made Machu Picchu the picture of Peru. The farming families who had been working the terraces when he arrived, the Richartes and the Álvarezes, became a line in someone else's story.

That is the version of the place most people arrive with: the lost city, found. Lizárraga's version is better and closer to the truth. Machu Picchu was never lost to the people who lived below it; it was simply a long climb from anywhere worth going. That is still the case, and it is the thing to plan around.

## How do Machu Picchu tickets work now?

You buy a named, timed ticket for one route, and you buy it early. Since June 2024 the site has been divided into three circuits and ten routes: Circuit 1, the panoramic one, which includes Machu Picchu Mountain and the upper terraces; Circuit 2, the classic walk through the city; and Circuit 3, which includes Huayna Picchu, the peak in every photograph, and Huchuy Picchu. Some routes, including the Inca bridge, the Sun Gate and the Great Cavern, open only in high season. Entry is by hourly slot from six in the morning to three in the afternoon.

For 2026 the daily limit is 5,600 people in high season, from 19 June to 2 November plus Easter and the New Year, and 4,500 the rest of the year. The routes that matter most are the smallest: Huayna Picchu and Machu Picchu Mountain are capped at 50 people a day each. Foreign adults pay S/152 for a standard route and S/200 for a route with Huayna Picchu, the Mountain or the Great Cavern, and since May 2026 there is an S/11 conservation fee on top.

The newest change is the one that matters for planning. In September 2026 the Ministry of Culture ended the old system of unpaid reservations, after an audit found that most of the tickets held by a handful of agencies were later cancelled. Tickets now carry the visitor's name and passport number, must be paid for within 15 minutes, and are sold only through the ministry's own site and authorised offices. The trial sold out every ticket. My rule: choose your dates, book the hotel, and buy the ticket the day the dates are on sale, with passport numbers for everyone in hand. If you want Huayna Picchu, decide now, not on the train.

## When should you go?

May to October, the dry season, if you want the clearest skies, and accept that June to August is when everyone else goes too. November to April is the rainy season. The months I would pick are April, May and October, either side of the peak, when the hillsides are still green and the queues are shorter.

Two dates to know. The Inca Trail closes every February, and its permits, about 500 a day including porters and guides, sell through licensed operators only and go five or six months ahead for the dry season. And Machu Picchu's own high season, as the ministry defines it for ticket limits, runs from 19 June to 2 November.

## Is it too crowded to be worth it?

No, but it is crowded enough that the order of your day matters. The New7Wonders foundation, which ran the 2007 vote that put Machu Picchu on its list, warned in September 2025 and again in May 2026 that overcrowding and the disputes over buses and tickets were putting the site's reputation at risk. Those warnings are fair, and the new ticket rules are partly an answer to them.

The answer for a traveller is the one Lizárraga had: be there when nobody else can be. The first entry slot at six and the last in the afternoon are the quiet ones, because the day-trippers from Cusco cannot reach the gate before the first trains arrive or stay after the last ones leave. That is the whole argument for sleeping at the bottom of the mountain, or on top of it.

## How do you get there, and how many nights do you need?

By train, and give it at least four nights in the region, five if you can. There is no road to Machu Picchu Pueblo, the town that used to be called Aguas Calientes. PeruRail's Vistadome runs from Ollantaytambo in the Sacred Valley in about an hour and a half; from May to December some trains also run from Poroy near Cusco. Inca Rail's First Class, with a balcony car and a three-course meal, also leaves from Ollantaytambo.

The train to book, if you are going to book one, is the **Belmond Hiram Bingham**, named, with some irony, for the man who erased Lizárraga's name. It runs daily except the last Sunday of each month from Poroy, a little over three hours, with a dining car, a bar car, live music, lunch with wine, a private bus up to the citadel and a guide, and tea at the Sanctuary Lodge before the ride home. Entry tickets are not included, so you still need to buy those. From the town, the shuttle bus to the gate takes about 25 to 30 minutes and costs foreign adults US$24 return.

Sleep low first. Cusco sits at about 3,400 metres and Machu Picchu at 2,430, and the Sacred Valley, between them, is the gentler place to arrive. Two nights in the valley, one or two at Machu Picchu, and two in Cusco at the end, when your lungs have caught up, is the order I would use.

## Where should you stay?

There are three places to sleep and each does a different job: Machu Picchu itself, for the ruins at the edges of the day; the Sacred Valley, for altitude and the Inca sites most people skip; and Cusco, for the city. A note on ratings: MICHELIN began awarding Keys in Peru in 2025, and Forbes Travel Guide rates only two of these hotels, both in Cusco.

### At the gate: Belmond Sanctuary Lodge

<figure><img src="https://img.belmond.com/f_auto/t_2580x1299/photos/mps/mps-gst-garden08.jpg" alt="The garden at Belmond Sanctuary Lodge, the only hotel at the entrance to Machu Picchu in Peru." loading="lazy"><figcaption>The garden at Belmond Sanctuary Lodge, Machu Picchu. Image courtesy of <a href="https://www.belmond.com/en/hotels/south-america/peru/sanctuary-lodge-machu-picchu" rel="noopener">Belmond</a>.</figcaption></figure>

**Belmond Sanctuary Lodge** is the only hotel at the entrance to Machu Picchu, and that is the whole case for it. It has 31 rooms, 29 rooms and two suites, on the Hiram Bingham road a three-minute walk from the gate and 25 minutes by road above the station, in a building that began as lodging for the archaeologists and researchers who worked on the site; Belmond has run it since 1999. You do not come for the rooms, which are small, with Deluxe rooms of 15 to 17 square metres. You come because when the last buses go down in the late afternoon you are still there, and in the morning you can be at the first slot before the first bus from town has arrived. The restaurant is Tampu, with Tampu Bar beside it; Tinkuy, the buffet, also serves day visitors at lunch, so eat at Tampu. The room to ask for is a One-Bedroom Suite, 35 to 45 square metres, the only rooms with any space. It is in the MICHELIN Guide without a Key and not rated by Forbes; let our advisors quote current rates. For one night at Machu Picchu, nothing else is close.

### In the cloud forest: Inkaterra Machu Picchu Pueblo Hotel

**Inkaterra Machu Picchu Pueblo Hotel** is the better hotel and the worse location, and for a family or a stay of two nights I would choose it. It is an eco-lodge of 80-odd adobe casitas, 85 rooms by MICHELIN's count, set in a five-hectare, 12-acre reserve of cloud forest at 2,020 metres, across the tracks from the Machu Picchu Pueblo station, with the town's bars and restaurants on one side and the shuttle buses to the citadel, leaving every 15 minutes, a short walk away. What makes it unique is the garden: Inkaterra counts 372 native orchid species and 317 bird species on the property, and the guided walks through them are the best way to spend an afternoon after a morning on the ruins. There is the UNU Spa, with an Andean sauna, and Café Inkaterra for Andean cooking. The rooms to ask for are the Villa Inkaterra, 293 square metres with a plunge pool and a butler, or, at a more sensible level, the Suite Inkaterra at 176 square metres. MICHELIN describes it as carbon-neutral and quiet, and calls the citadel "a half-day trip" from the door, which is exactly the right expectation. Let our advisors quote current rates.

### On the river: Sumaq Machu Picchu

**Sumaq Machu Picchu Hotel** is the town hotel for travellers who want a proper hotel rather than a lodge. It sits on the Vilcanota, the river Lizárraga drowned in, at the edge of Machu Picchu Pueblo, with about 60 rooms, and it is a member of Small Luxury Hotels of the World. Qunuq, its restaurant, does Andean tasting menus, and the Imperial Suite with River View, with a whirlpool and a butler, is the one to ask for. It is in the MICHELIN Guide without a Key.

### For two Keys in the Sacred Valley: Inkaterra Hacienda Urubamba

**Inkaterra Hacienda Urubamba** holds Two MICHELIN Keys, the highest award near Machu Picchu, and it is the hotel I would choose for the first nights in the valley. It is a 36-room hacienda on a hillside of about 100 acres at kilometre 63 of the road between Urubamba and Pisac, in a contemporary hacienda style with colonial furniture and Inca masks, and its kitchen is earth-to-table. The casitas are 45 square metres each, and a family or a group of friends should ask for the Urubamba Owners Quarters, 190 square metres for up to six. It is far enough from Cusco to sleep low, and close enough to Pisac, Moray and the salt pans at Maras to fill two days without a long drive. Let our advisors quote current rates.

### For the all-inclusive: explora Valle Sagrado

**explora Valle Sagrado** is the one for travellers who want the valley handled for them. It holds one MICHELIN Key and sits on a 32-hectare historic estate at Urquillos, near Urubamba, at 2,900 metres, about an hour and a half to two hours from Cusco airport. MICHELIN notes that it was built on an Inca site uncovered during construction, and the Pumacahua Spa is in a restored 17th-century manor house. There are 50 rooms, 44 Standard rooms of about 40 square metres and six Suites of about 53, and the price is all-inclusive: meals, the open bar and airport transfers. explora recommends five nights, and the lodge is built for them; its multi-lodge programmes start at US$5,540 per person for eight nights. If you want the valley planned for you from the moment you land, this is the one.

### For the train from the garden: Belmond Hotel Rio Sagrado

**Belmond Hotel Rio Sagrado** has one MICHELIN Key and one feature nobody else has: its own station. It is 21 rooms and two villas, plus a Signature Villa, in gardens on the river at kilometre 75.8 of the road from Urubamba to Ollantaytambo, five minutes from Urubamba and 70 minutes from Cusco, and the train to Machu Picchu stops at the bottom of the garden; Belmond sells the combination as "From Your Room to Machu Picchu" on the Hiram Bingham. The Mayu Willka spa is good, and the meal to ask for is the pachamanca, the Andean feast cooked underground on hot stones. Muju is the restaurant, El Jardín the casual one. Ask for a Garden Junior Suite, 92 square metres, or for a family the Signature Villa, 330 square metres for up to seven. For a small, quiet valley hotel that turns the train into part of the stay, it is the one.

### Also good in the valley: Sol y Luna

**Sol y Luna** is a Relais & Châteaux hotel of 43 stone casitas on ten hectares near Urubamba, in the MICHELIN Guide without a Key, and the one I would pick for a couple who want warmth and colour over polish. Its profits fund the Sol y Luna Association's school programmes for valley children, which is a good reason to stay, and the Premium Casita, 1,291 square feet with a terrace whirlpool, two wood fireplaces and murals, is the room to ask for.

### In Cusco, for the end of the trip: Palacio Nazarenas

<figure><img src="https://img.belmond.com/f_auto/t_2580x1299/photos/naz/naz-gst-pool28.jpg" alt="The heated outdoor pool in the courtyard of Palacio Nazarenas, A Belmond Hotel, in a former convent in Cusco, Peru." loading="lazy"><figcaption>The courtyard pool at Palacio Nazarenas, Cusco. **Palacio Nazarenas, A Belmond Hotel** is a Forbes Travel Guide Four-Star with one MICHELIN Key, and it is where I would end the trip. It is 55 suites in a building that has been an Inca site, a 16th-century palace, a Jesuit college and a convent, on the Plazoleta Nazarenas in the historic centre; Belmond reopened it as a hotel in 2012. It has a heated outdoor pool in the courtyard and a spa where, MICHELIN notes, one treatment room has a glass floor over Inca walls. Mauka, the restaurant, is led by Pía León, one of Lima's best-known chefs, and Senzo is the bar. Ask for a Signature Suite, 69 to 130 square metres; the Master Suite, with carved ceilings and a corner terrace over the square, is the one MICHELIN singles out. Its sister nearby, **Monasterio, A Belmond Hotel**, is the bigger, older alternative, also Forbes Four-Star with one MICHELIN Key: 117 rooms in a seminary of 1595 built over an Inca palace, with a baroque chapel, a 300-year-old cedar in the courtyard and rooms with added oxygen for the altitude.

## A six-night plan, from the valley to the citadel

**Nights one and two, the Sacred Valley.** Fly into Cusco and drive straight down to the valley, an hour or two depending on the hotel, to Inkaterra Hacienda Urubamba, explora or Rio Sagrado. Spend the first day doing very little, and the second at Pisac, Moray and the salt pans at Maras.

**Night three, Machu Picchu.** Take the train from Ollantaytambo, or from Rio Sagrado's own platform, and arrive in time for a late-afternoon slot. Sleep at the Sanctuary Lodge or Inkaterra.

**Day four, the morning entry.** Be at the gate for the first slot, with Huayna Picchu or the Mountain if you have it, and find the Temple of the Three Windows. Lizárraga's name is not on the wall any more, but you will know where it was. Take the afternoon train back towards Cusco.

**Nights four to six, Cusco.** Two or three nights at Palacio Nazarenas or Monasterio, with the city, Sacsayhuamán and Mauka for dinner, before the flight home.

It is not the only way to do it, but it puts the altitude in the right order and the ruins at the right time of day. Lizárraga had the place to himself. The closest you can come now is the first hour of the morning, and a hotel that gets you there before the buses do. That is the part worth planning, and it is the part we plan.
