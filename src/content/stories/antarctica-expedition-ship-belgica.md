Every Antarctic season starts with the same question, usually around now: which ship? The brochures all promise the same penguins and the same ice, and the prices run from merely expensive to the cost of a small car. The honest answer is that the ship decides more about this trip than almost anything else you book, because in Antarctica the ship is the hotel, the restaurant, the transport and the limit on how long you get to stand on the ice.

I like to start somewhere else, though, with a small Belgian ship that got there first. In January 1898 the Belgica, a converted Norwegian sealer under Adrien de Gerlache, sailed into a long channel off the west coast of the Antarctic Peninsula and spent the next few weeks mapping it, landing on its shores and handing out names. Cuverville Island, Andvord Bay, the Lemaire Channel: if you sail the classic Peninsula route this season, you will spend some of your best days in places the Belgica named.

Then the expedition did something nobody had done before, and nobody wanted to do again. It spent a whole winter frozen into the pack ice, in the dark, with scurvy setting in. The crew that came home included a future conqueror of the South Pole and a doctor who would later be jailed for fraud. Here is what happened, what you can still see, and how I would choose your ship.

## What was the Belgica, and why does it matter now?

It was the first expedition to spend a winter in Antarctica. The Belgica left Antwerp on August 16, 1897, with an international crew: Belgians and Norwegians, two Polish scientists, the Romanian zoologist Emil Racoviță, and an American doctor, Frederick Cook. The first mate was a 25-year-old Norwegian named Roald Amundsen. The ship even had a cat, called Nansen.

The voyage began with a loss. On January 22, 1898, a young Norwegian sailor, Carl Wiencke, was washed overboard and drowned. Days later the Belgica reached the coast of Graham Land, and over the following weeks it worked its way down the strait between the Palmer Archipelago and the mainland. Gerlache called it Belgica Strait. It was later renamed the Gerlache Strait in his honor, and Wiencke Island, off the Peninsula's west coast, still carries the lost sailor's name.

## What happened when the ice closed in?

The ship was trapped for a year, and the crew nearly lost their minds. After crossing the Antarctic Circle on February 15, 1898, Gerlache pushed south and west into the pack of the Bellingshausen Sea, and by the first days of March the Belgica was held fast. Whether he did it on purpose, to claim the first Antarctic winter, is still argued over. Through the depths of the winter the sun did not rise at all.

That is the part people underestimate. In June the young Belgian officer Émile Danco died. Scurvy spread through the crew. Two men showed signs of serious mental illness, and one of them was committed to an asylum when the ship got home.

What saved them was the doctor's insistence on fresh meat. Cook was convinced that fresh meat kept scurvy away, and he had the crew eat seal and penguin every day. It was unpopular. Amundsen, almost alone, declared the penguin excellent. Gerlache held out for a time and then joined in, and by the end of July the crew were living mostly on penguin, and recovering. According to Smithsonian magazine, the men found they could draw penguins to the ship by playing a cornet, and Gerlache paid a franc for every bird brought in alive.

## How did they get out, and what became of them?

They cut their way out by hand. When open water finally appeared a few hundred metres off in the southern summer, the crew spent weeks sawing and blasting a channel toward it with saws, pickaxes and tonite explosive. On February 15, 1899, the channel opened and the Belgica began to move. It took nearly another month to work about seven miles through the pack, and on March 14, 1899, the ship was free. It reached Punta Arenas at the end of that month and Antwerp in November.

The survivors went on to very different futures. Amundsen reached the South Pole on December 14, 1911. Cook later claimed to have reached the North Pole first, a claim that has never been accepted, and was imprisoned for fraud over oil company shares. Gerlache's legacy is quieter: it is on the map, and you will see it from the deck.

## Which places on today's route did the Belgica name?

More than most travelers realize. The Gerlache Strait runs for almost 200 kilometres between the Palmer Archipelago and the Danco Coast, which is named after the officer who died in the ice. The classic Peninsula landings sit along it or just off it, and several are Belgica names:

**Cuverville Island**, in the Errera Channel, was discovered by the expedition and named for a French vice-admiral. It holds one of the largest gentoo penguin colonies on the Peninsula, about 7,000 breeding pairs, and it is on a great many itineraries.

**Andvord Bay** was discovered by the expedition and named after Rolf Andvord, the Belgian consul in Christiania, now Oslo. Neko Harbour, the small bay on its eastern shore, has a gentoo colony of about 250 pairs and is a landing on the Antarctic mainland itself rather than an island.

**The Lemaire Channel** was explored by Gerlache in 1898 and named for Charles Lemaire, a Belgian who had explored the Congo and never went near Antarctica. It is about 11 kilometres long and around 600 metres wide at its narrowest, between Booth Island and the mainland, and it is usually the southernmost point of a standard voyage. Expedition crews call it Kodak Gap. Ice closes it on some days, which is the first lesson in how this trip works: the plan is a wish list, and the expedition leader decides each morning.

## When should you go?

November to March, and the month decides what you see. November is early season: the snow is clean, the pack ice is breaking up, and penguins are courting and building nests, with up to 22 hours of daylight. December and January bring the longest light, the first chicks and, in January, usually the calmest weather. February and March are the whale months, when humpbacks, minkes and orcas feed in the channels; by March the light is down to about 12 hours and the season is closing.

My rule: November if you are a photographer, December or January for a first trip or a family, February if whales matter more to you than penguin chicks.

## How big should the ship be?

Under 200 guests if you can, because of how landings work. IAATO, the association of Antarctic tour operators, does not allow more than 100 people ashore at one site at the same time, requires one guide for every 20 passengers, and bars ships carrying more than 500 passengers from landing at all. On a ship of 250, landings are run in shifts and you get less time on each beach. On a ship under 150, everyone can be ashore at once.

The second decision is the Drake Passage, the two days of open water between South America and the Peninsula. Most voyages sail it from Ushuaia, and on a bad day it is everything you have heard. Fly-cruises skip it, flying from southern Chile to King George Island in the South Shetlands and boarding there. They cost more per day and give you fewer days on the Peninsula, but for anyone prone to seasickness, they are the answer.

**Silver Endeavour** is the most polished way to skip the Drake, and it is a Forbes Four-Star ship for 2026. It carries 220 guests in 100 suites, all with a veranda, the smallest at 355 square feet, with a crew-to-guest ratio of about one to one and a butler for every suite. What nobody else has is the arrival: Silversea flies its own charter from Santiago to Puerto Williams on Navarino Island, puts you up for a night at The Cormorant at 55 South, its new 150-room hotel, which opened this autumn and is open only to Silversea's fly-cruise guests, then flies you across the Drake to King George Island to board. Dinner on board is French at La Dame, Italian at Il Terrazzino or casual at The Grill, and a stabilized Shotover camera system on the ship can film wildlife up to three miles away and show it in the Expedition Lounge. The seven-day round trip from King George Island spends three days on the Peninsula, plus the Antarctic Sound and the South Shetlands; the December 1 to 7, 2026 departure is from $23,900 per person. For couples who want Antarctica without the crossing, and without giving up a butler, nothing else does it as smoothly.

**National Geographic Endurance** is the one I would choose for the expedition itself. Lindblad's first purpose-built polar ship carries 138 guests in 76 cabins, has a PC5 ice class, and was built on Ulstein's X-Bow hull, which rides the Drake more smoothly and with less spray than a traditional bow. The signature extra is the glass igloos on deck, where you can spend a night under the Antarctic sky. Lindblad sails with National Geographic, and its naturalist teams are the reason people book it; the main dining room, Restaurant 270°, has wrap-around views. Ask for a Category 7 suite, about 430 square feet with a balcony and priority on the igloos. The 12-day Journey to Antarctica starts with a night in Buenos Aires or Santiago and a charter to Ushuaia, then sails the Drake; let our advisors quote current fares, which move through the season. For travelers who want their days led by the best naturalists rather than the best sommelier, this is the ship.

**Scenic Eclipse II** is for people who want the biggest toys. The 2023 ship has 114 suites and room for 228 guests, but Scenic caps it at 200 in Antarctica. It carries two helicopters and a submersible, which are extra and weather-dependent, and its restaurants include the French Lumière and Koko's, an Asian-fusion room. The suite to ask for is the Two-Bedroom Owner's Penthouse, 247 square metres. Its Antarctica in Depth voyages run 12 to 13 days, with a hotel night in Buenos Aires, an included charter to Ushuaia and five to seven days on the Peninsula. For a family or a group of friends who want something nobody else on the beach will do, I would book this.

**Seabourn Pursuit** is the ultra-luxury ship with submarines. Delivered in 2023 with a Polar Class 6 hull, it carries 264 guests in 132 veranda suites, the largest of the five, so expect landings in shifts, and it has two custom submarines that can dive to 300 metres. The expedition team numbers 26, and dining runs from The Restaurant to The Colonnade and the sushi counter at The Club. The two-storey Wintergarden Suites are the ones to ask for. Seabourn lists Neko Harbour and Cuverville among its Antarctic landings, and The Great White Continent runs 11 nights from Buenos Aires with a flight to Ushuaia; the March 13, 2027 departure is from $16,098 per person in a Veranda Suite. For travelers who care about the suite and the service as much as the landings, it is the best value of the five.

**Le Commandant Charcot** is the one for the Belgica's own waters, and it means waiting a year. Ponant's hybrid-electric icebreaker has a Polar Class 2 hull, the highest of any passenger ship here, 245 guests in 123 suites and staterooms, all with a balcony or terrace, and in 2021 it became the first passenger ship to reach the North Pole. Its fine-dining restaurant, Nuna, works with Alain Ducasse; the casual room is Sila. The Owner's Suite has a terrace of about 2,000 square feet with a hot tub. It is in the Arctic this winter, but in the 2027 to 2028 season it returns with In the Wake of Jean-Baptiste Charcot, 16 days from Ushuaia toward the Bellingshausen Sea, where the Belgica spent its winter, departing December 2 and December 28, 2027, from $26,030 per person. For anyone who has already seen the Peninsula, or who wants to go where the classic ships cannot, this is the one to hold a cabin on now.

## What the Belgica still teaches

The Belgica's crew went south to fill in a blank on the map and came home having nearly died of darkness and bad food. Every comfort on these ships, the heated boot rooms, the fresh fruit at breakfast, the doctor down the corridor, is an answer to a lesson they learned the hard way. It is worth thinking about when you are standing on the deck in the Gerlache Strait with a coffee, looking at a coastline that a small Belgian ship mapped by hand.
