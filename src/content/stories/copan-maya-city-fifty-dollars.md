In the autumn of 1839, a lawyer turned travel writer named John Lloyd Stephens rode a mule into a forest in western Honduras and found a square column of stone about fourteen feet high, carved on all four sides. His guide called it an idol. Stephens had come to Central America on paper as President Van Buren's envoy, sent to a federation that was, at that moment, collapsing into civil war. He was not much interested in the federation. He and the British architect Frederick Catherwood had come for the ruins, and the city around that column, he wrote, "lay before us like a shattered bark in the midst of the ocean."

What he did next is the part that still delights me. He found the man who controlled the land, a Don José María, and made him an offer. "I paid 50 dollars for Copan," Stephens wrote, adding that Don José María thought him only a fool for it.

The plan was to float the city to New York and put it in a museum. It was a very American plan and it failed for a very simple reason: there was no river near enough to carry it. So Stephens wrote, Catherwood drew, and the book they published in 1841, *Incidents of Travel in Central America, Chiapas, and Yucatan*, became an immediate best seller and made the Maya a public fascination.

The honest answer on Copán is that it is one of the finest Maya sites anywhere, that it is smaller than Tikal and better carved, and that getting there asks more of you than most luxury trips will. Here is what Stephens actually bought, what to see, and how I would plan it.

## What did Stephens actually buy for fifty dollars?

Less than the legend suggests. By Stephens's own account, Don José María did not own the land at all. He held a lease on about 6,000 acres at 80 dollars a year, with three years still to run, from another owner. Stephens paid the fifty dollars anyway, and the tradition that he "bought Copán" has followed him ever since, which is exactly the outcome a man who wrote best sellers for a living would have wanted. What the money conveyed in law is a fair question. In practice it bought him the right to clear the trees and draw what was under them.

The local reaction, in his telling, was that the two foreigners must be practicing some dark art to find buried treasure. Catherwood, meanwhile, was standing in rain and mud among chiggers and mosquitoes, working with a camera lucida, an optical drawing aid, to record the carved stelae as exactly as the instrument allowed. One of those plates, a stela with its fists clenched against its chest and the forest closing in behind it, appeared in the 1841 book. It is still the best introduction to the site.

Stephens could not read the carvings. Nobody could. But he looked at them and reached a conclusion that was radical for 1839: the cities had not been built by Egyptians, Hebrews or survivors of Atlantis. They were built by the peoples of the region itself, or their not-very-distant ancestors. Then he wrote the line that makes the rest of this story worth telling, about the city's history being recorded on its monuments, and asked: "Who shall read them?"

## Can anyone read the glyphs now?

Yes, and that is the reason to go. It took about a century of work by many people, but scholars can now read most of what survives, and Stephens's guess that the inscriptions recorded royal history turned out to be right. Copán's texts name a dynasty founded by K'inich Yax K'uk' Mo', who reigned from 426 to about 437, and running through sixteen rulers, all of whom are carved in order around a single stone known as Altar Q. NOVA's account credits the epigrapher David Stuart with reading the founder's name there in 1986.

The story those texts tell is a good one. The thirteenth king, Waxaklajuun Ub'aah K'awiil, usually called 18 Rabbit, reigned for more than four decades and filled the Great Plaza with the deeply carved stelae you will walk among. In 738, the ruler of Quiriguá, a smaller neighboring center, captured and beheaded him. Copán survived it, rebuilt and carried on for most of another century.

## What should you see, and in what order?

Be at the gate when it opens, go to the Hieroglyphic Stairway first, and save the museum for last. The stairway climbs more than sixty steps up Temple 26 and carries more than 2,000 glyphs, the longest known Maya inscription, recording the dynasty's history from the early fifth century to the mid-eighth. It also has one of archaeology's better embarrassments attached to it. Peabody Museum excavators uncovered it in 1893, with many of the steps slumped from their original positions, and the Carnegie Institution rebuilt it between 1936 and 1942, before anyone could read it. The Peabody says the result is about 65 percent out of order. Epigraphers have since virtually reconstructed about 71 percent of the original sequence, with help from glass plate negatives taken in the 1890s. So you are looking at the longest text the Maya left, and much of it is shuffled.

Then walk the Great Plaza and 18 Rabbit's stelae, and the ball court beside them. The site takes about three hours; add Las Sepulturas, a short tuk-tuk ride along the highway, which your main ticket covers. Reports put the hours at 8 a.m. to 4 p.m. daily and prices, this year, at US$20 for the park, US$10 for the Sculpture Museum and US$15 for the tunnels, but check with the Honduran Institute of Anthropology and History before you travel. Hire a guide at the entrance and agree the language, route and price first.

Do not skip the museum. In 1989, tunneling beneath Temple 16, Ricardo Agurcia Fasquelle found Rosalila, a late sixth-century temple that the Maya themselves had buried inside a later pyramid, painted facade and all, preserved on all four sides. The museum's central atrium holds a full-scale replica in its original red, green, yellow and cream. The tunnels are for people who want to stand inside the pyramid; the museum is where you see what the Maya actually looked at. If you have to choose, choose the museum.

## When should you go, and is it safe?

November to April, and yes, with planning. The dry season gives clearer weather and firmer paths, and November to January is the coolest stretch, with daytime highs below about 26°C (80°F). The wet months are not a disaster, since rain tends to arrive in the afternoon, which is one more reason to start at the gate.

Safety is the conversation to have honestly. The U.S. State Department, in its August 2026 advisory, rates Honduras Level 3, Reconsider Travel, citing crime and terrorism, and a State of Exception has been in force in most municipalities since December 2022. The highest crime is in Francisco Morazán, Cortés and Colón departments and on the north coast. The advisory does not mention Copán, and travel writers describe the town and the ruins as one of the calmer parts of the mainland. But Cortés contains San Pedro Sula, the usual airport, which is why the road matters. My rule: no driving after dark, private transfer both ways, and read the current advisory the week you book.

## How do you get there?

Slowly. From San Pedro Sula it is at least four hours on winding roads, and I would not self-drive it. From Guatemala you come through the El Florido crossing, about 14 kilometres from town; the shared shuttle from Antigua is scheduled for about seven hours and has been known to take ten. Pairing Copán with Guatemala makes more sense to me than a stand-alone trip from Honduras: you arrive overland, and you have already done the hard part of getting to the region. Allow two nights at the minimum, three if you want the macaws and the hills.

## What else is worth the time?

The macaws, by a wide margin. Macaw Mountain, about 2.4 kilometres from the town center, is a sanctuary for rescued birds that has run a rewilding program since 2011 with the World Parrot Trust and the Asociación Copán, releasing scarlet macaws into the valley. Look up on the path to the ruins and you will often see the descendants of that project flying over a city their ancestors were carved on. Luna Jaguar hot springs, about 50 minutes north by road, is the other half-day I would give it, along with a coffee farm visit at Finca El Cisne if you are staying on.

## Where should you stay?

Hacienda San Lucas, if you can get a room. It is a century-old family hacienda in the hills above the valley, about a mile south of town, restored by its owner, Flavia Cueva, as an eight-room adobe inn on some 300 acres, candlelit in the evenings, with food cooked on a log stove, the small Maya site of Los Sapos on the property and the temple tops visible from the garden. It is simple rather than luxurious and is for travelers who would take character over a spa; if you want to be in town, La Casa Rosada, a small Spanish colonial-style inn two blocks from the central park with steam showers in the bathrooms, is the choice. Both are small, owner-run houses rather than grand hotels, and that suits Copán: the reason to come is the valley and the ruins, and these are the two places that put you closest to both. Rooms are few and rates change with the season, so let our advisors quote current prices and hold the room you want.

## The fifty dollars, in the end

Neither man got to enjoy it for long. Stephens went on to help build the Panama Railroad, caught malaria there in the spring of 1852 and died in New York that October at forty-six. Catherwood went down with the steamship *Arctic* in the North Atlantic on September 27, 1854. The city they tried to buy and ship home is still standing in the valley where they found it, and it is still being read. Fifty dollars was a bargain. The better deal was the one the rest of us got, which was the book.
