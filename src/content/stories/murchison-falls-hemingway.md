In January 1954, Ernest Hemingway gave his wife a Christmas present that arrived a few weeks late: a sightseeing flight from Nairobi to Murchison Falls, in Uganda, to see the Nile squeeze through a slot in the rock and throw itself into the gorge below. He was on his second African safari, and on assignment for *Look* magazine. Their pilot, Roy Marsh, took the small Cessna low over the falls.

On Saturday, January 23, the plane struck a disused telegraph wire and came down in the scrub a few hundred yards from the Nile, about three miles below the falls. Nobody was killed. Mary had broken ribs. The three of them camped beside the river for the night. The next morning a launch full of tourists, on its way up to see the falls, found them on the bank and carried them downriver to Butiaba, on the shore of Lake Albert, where a de Havilland Rapide was waiting to fly them to Entebbe.

The Rapide crashed on take-off and caught fire. The pilot, Reggie Cartwright, got Mary out, and Hemingway, finding the door jammed, broke it open with his head. By then an RAF search pilot had flown over the first wreck and seen no sign of life, the news had gone around the world, and newspapers on several continents had printed his obituary. He lived to read them.

The honest answer on Murchison Falls is that it is still one of the great sights in Africa, that most people see it badly, from a viewpoint, in an hour, and that it rewards the traveler who gives it three days and comes at it from the river, the way Hemingway's rescuers did. Here is what happened, what to see, and where I would put you.

## What actually happened to Hemingway at Murchison Falls?

Two crashes in two days, and injuries he never fully shook off. The first was close to harmless. A British airline captain flying an Argonaut to Khartoum went 80 to 100 miles off course to look for the missing Cessna, found it among the scrub trees, read its registration letters clearly and remarked that "it looked like the chap did a neat job of landing the aircraft." The search had begun when the plane failed to arrive at Masindi to refuel.

The second crash was the one that mattered. The Rapide, an East African Airways biplane, burned completely, along with the luggage and the receipts for the trip. His own account, in a letter written to his lawyer a few months afterward, lists a right arm "burned to the bone," a ruptured right kidney, injuries to his liver and spleen, and the line "I am weak from so much internal bleeding." Other accounts add crushed vertebrae and head injuries. That letter sold at auction in 2023 for $237,055.

The obituaries are the part everyone remembers. The injuries are the part that shaped the rest of his life. Later that year he won the Nobel Prize in Literature. He did not go to Stockholm for it. At the banquet on December 10, 1954, the American ambassador to Sweden, John C. Cabot, read his acceptance speech for him, and the Swedish Academy explained that he was absent because of ill health. "Writing, at its best, is a lonely life," the speech said.

A photograph of the first wreck, the little Cessna in the bush with three unidentified men beside it, is in the Hemingway collection at the John F. Kennedy Presidential Library in Boston. It is worth looking up before you go.

## What is Murchison Falls, and why is it worth the trip?

It is the point where the whole of the Victoria Nile is forced through a gap in the rock about six or seven metres wide and drops more than 40 metres into a pool known as the Devil's Cauldron. Samuel and Florence Baker were the first Europeans known to have seen it, in the 1860s, and Baker named it for Sir Roderick Murchison, then president of the Royal Geographical Society. You will still hear it called Kabalega Falls, the name it carried for a time in the 1970s, after a king of Bunyoro.

The park around it is Uganda's largest, more than 3,800 square kilometres, and among its oldest: the land was first protected as a game reserve early in the last century and became a national park in 1952. The Nile cuts it in two. North of the river is open savanna with giraffe, buffalo, Uganda kob, elephant and lion; along the river itself are hippos and crocodiles in numbers; and Uganda Wildlife Authority counts more than 450 bird species in the park. It is a full safari, which surprises people who think of Uganda only as gorilla country.

What has changed since 1954 is the reason to come. Hemingway was in East Africa to hunt. The way to see Murchison now is with binoculars and a boat, and that is the trip I would plan.

## How should you see the falls?

From the river first, then on foot. The single best half day in the park is the launch from Paraa, the ferry crossing in the middle of the park, upstream about 17 kilometres to the foot of the falls. Uganda Wildlife Authority runs it, and private operators run smaller boats. It takes about three hours there and back, past pods of hippos, crocodiles on the sandbanks and, in the dry months, elephants and buffalo coming down to drink. This is the stretch of river where Hemingway's plane came down, and the same route his rescuers took.

Here is the part people miss: you do not have to go back on the boat. Ask to be dropped below the falls and walk up through the Fajao gorge to the top with a ranger, which UWA recommends over driving to the viewpoint. At the top you stand beside the slot itself, which is the view no boat or aircraft gives you. Have your driver meet you there. Do the boat in the morning, when the light on the water is kinder and the animals are moving, and the walk before the heat builds.

If you have a second day on the river, take the opposite direction. The delta cruise runs downstream from Paraa toward Lake Albert, about four to five hours, into papyrus channels where the shoebill, the strange, grey, prehistoric-looking stork that birders cross the world for, is possible but never promised. Take the morning departure.

## When should you go?

In the dry seasons: December to late February, and June to September. The tracks are firmer, the grass is shorter, and the animals concentrate along the river, which is exactly where the boat goes. Uganda Wildlife Authority says birding is best from January to March, when visitor numbers are also at their lowest, and that is the window I would choose for a first visit. The wet months are not a disaster, but some of the northern tracks get heavy and the game spreads out.

## How do you get there, and how long do you need?

Three nights, two at the minimum. By road, the park is about five hours from Kampala, and the drive is part of the trip if you stop for the rhinos at Ziwa Rhino Sanctuary on the way. By air, there are scheduled and charter flights from Entebbe or Kajjansi to Pakuba airfield inside the park. My rule: fly in if your time in Uganda is short or you are combining Murchison with the gorillas in Bwindi, and drive if you want to see the country.

On fees, Uganda Wildlife Authority's tariff for January 2027 to June 2028 keeps the numbers travelers have been paying: US$45 a day for park entry for foreign non-residents, valid for 24 hours, US$30 for the launch trip to the bottom of the falls and US$15 for the hike to the top. Your lodge or our advisors will usually fold these into the price, but check them the week you book.

## Where should you stay?

Close to the river, and in my view with the people who named their camp for the man in this story. **Papa's Camp**, from the safari company WildPlaces Africa, is named for Ernest "Papa" Hemingway: five ensuite tents of about 36 square metres in a grove of fig trees on the south bank of the Nile, each with an outdoor shower and a view of the water, in a newly designated low-use zone of the park that it shares with only one other lodge. Game drives, night drives, fishing and walks with an armed ranger start from camp, and the top of the falls is about an hour and a half away by road. It is small, old-fashioned in the good sense and right for travelers who want the river to themselves.

The other lodge in that zone is **Kulu Ora**, which WildPlaces opened in March 2026 on a stretch of the Nile that divides around a forested island. It has 11 canvas-walled suites, including a two-bedroom suite for families, each with timber floors, a private plunge pool and a copper bathtub facing the river, plus a lap pool, a spa above the water and a library chosen by the company's co-founder, Pamela Wright. It runs on solar power, and apart from the pools it was built to be taken down within a week and leave no trace. A conservation levy of $25 per bed night goes to the park and its neighbors. For a honeymoon or a first safari in Uganda, this is the one I would book.

If you would rather an established lodge with its own boats on the river, **Baker's Lodge**, on the south bank and named for the explorer who named the falls, is the one I would choose. Rooms are few at all three, so let our advisors quote current rates and hold the dates you want.

## The obituaries, in the end

Hemingway spent the weeks after the crashes recovering, first in Nairobi and later in Venice, and the Nobel came at the end of the year. The falls he saw for a few minutes from the air are still doing exactly what they were doing that Saturday. The difference is that you can see them properly now, from the river and then from the edge of the slot, and fly home without making the papers.
