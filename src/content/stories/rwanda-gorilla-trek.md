In 1921, Carl Akeley climbed into the Virunga volcanoes, in what was then the Belgian Congo, to collect mountain gorillas for the American Museum of Natural History. He was the most celebrated taxidermist alive, the man often credited with inventing modern taxidermy and the museum diorama, and he had come to bring gorillas back to New York.

He did. But he also watched them. Akeley became one of the first people to describe mountain gorillas as intelligent, social animals, and on the way down the mountain he wrote that he feared they would be lost before anyone acted. He called the slopes of Mount Mikeno "the most beautiful place in all of Africa."

So he acted. Akeley persuaded King Albert I of Belgium to set the gorillas' mountains aside as a sanctuary, and in 1925 Albert National Park was created, the first national park in Africa. He went back in 1926 to study the animals he had helped protect, died of illness at his camp at Kabara, and was buried a short walk from it, under the forest.

The collector became the gorillas' first advocate. A century later, his mountains are where you go to spend an hour a few meters from a silverback. Here is how to do it.

## Why Rwanda?

Mountain gorillas live in only two places on earth: the Virunga volcanoes, shared by Rwanda, Uganda and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and Uganda's Bwindi forest. Rwanda's side of the Virungas is Volcanoes National Park, and it is the easiest gorilla trek in Africa to plan well.

Kigali is a short flight from most hubs and a few hours' drive from the park. Every trek starts from one headquarters at Kinigi. And the lodges on the park edge are among the best in Africa. If this is your first gorilla trek, Rwanda is the one to book.

## How do the permits work?

Start here, because everything else depends on it. A gorilla permit in Rwanda costs **US$1,500** per person for visitors from outside Africa. It covers park entry, a ranger-led trek and up to **one hour** with a habituated gorilla family.

Supply is deliberately tight. Twelve gorilla groups are open to visitors and only **eight permits a day** are issued for each, so fewer than a hundred people see gorillas in Rwanda on any given day. Trekkers must be at least **15**. Permits can be booked through the Rwanda Development Board, but most travelers have their advisor secure them alongside the lodge, which matters in the busiest months.

The price is the point. Permit revenue pays for rangers and the communities that border the park, which is exactly the kind of protection Akeley was asking for.

## Where should you stay?

Two lodges stand out, and both are growing back the forest the gorillas need.

**Wilderness Bisate** has six forest villas set inside an eroded volcanic cone beside Kinigi, the park headquarters. The villas are built in the style of traditional Rwandan royal architecture and look out to Mounts Bisoke, Karisimbi and Mikeno, Akeley's mountain. Wilderness has been replanting indigenous trees on the property since 2017. Book Bisate when you want the conservation story to be part of the stay, not a line in the brochure.

**Singita Kwitonda Lodge** sits right against the park boundary, with a small number of suites, each with a heated plunge pool and its own wellness area. It holds two MICHELIN Keys, and the adjoining Kataza House, a four-bedroom villa with a private chef and butler, is the strongest option in Rwanda for families and groups. Singita is also replanting the land around the lodge to extend gorilla habitat. Book it when you want the most polished stay on the mountain.

## Three days on the volcanoes: the itinerary

**Day 1: Kigali to Kinigi.** Land in Kigali and drive north through terraced hills to the park. Arrive in time for a slow afternoon and an early night.

**Day 2: The trek.** Report to park headquarters at dawn, where rangers assign you to a gorilla family. The walk might take forty minutes or five hours. Then the hour itself: no glass, no vehicle, a family going about its morning around you.

**Day 3: A second trek, or a different mountain.** Many travelers buy a second permit, because no two families behave alike. Others hike Mount Bisoke to its crater lake or track golden monkeys in the bamboo.

Add Nyungwe or Akagera to turn the trip into a full Rwanda circuit. Between November and May, that combination also earns a permit discount.

## What Akeley would still recognize

<figure><img src="https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1783739415051-642a46f2d91e?auto=format&fit=crop&w=1600&q=80" alt="The hand of a mountain gorilla resting among green bamboo leaves." loading="lazy"><figcaption>Bamboo forest, where most treks end. Image courtesy of Unsplash.</figcaption></figure>

The borders have changed, and the park now carries a different name on each side of them, but the mountains are the ones Akeley climbed. Mikeno still rises over the forest on the Congo side, and the bamboo still closes in a few steps off the trail. What he would not recognize is the ending. The animals he feared would vanish are still there, because people decided they should be, and because travelers keep paying to see them alive.
