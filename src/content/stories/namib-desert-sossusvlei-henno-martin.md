In May 1940, with Germany overrunning Holland and the news on the wireless getting worse by the day, two German geologists in South West Africa, the territory that is now Namibia, loaded a truck with supplies, a radio and a dog, and drove into the Namib Desert. Henno Martin was thirty and Hermann Korn was thirty-two. They had left Germany in 1935 and had been working in the territory ever since, prospecting for water. Now German men around them were disappearing behind the wire of internment camps, and they had decided, half joking years earlier, that "if war comes we'll spend it in the desert."

They meant it. They drove at night, let the air out of their tyres to cross the sand of the Swakop riverbed, and went down into the Kuiseb Canyon, a gorge cut through the gravel plains on the northern edge of the dunes, where nobody would think to look. The dog was called Otto, Martin explained, because the name looks the same fore and aft, and as a puppy so did he. They stayed for about two and a half years, living on springbok, gemsbok, klipspringer and the occasional fish, and listening to the war on a radio run off the truck's battery. Martin later wrote it all down, and the book, translated into English as The Sheltering Desert, is the best thing ever written about the Namib.

It is also, which surprises people, a book about two men who were happy. Their reason for going, Martin wrote, was that "this was not our war," and the desert gave them something better to do than fight in it. That is roughly the case for going today. The Namib's dunes are the colour of paprika, and the best lodges in it are as good as anything in Africa. But it is a big place, the road distances are long, and where you sleep decides whether you see the dunes at dawn or at noon. Here is how I would plan it, and where I would put you.

## What happened in the Kuiseb Canyon?

Two men and a dog lived in it, more or less undetected, from May 1940 until September 1942. Their first home was an overhang in the canyon wall they called Carp Cliff, after the carp they found in the Kuiseb's water holes, probably washed down from farm dams upriver in the rains of 1934. Martin describes a living room with two folding chairs, a metal trunk for a table and the radio in a petrol case, and a stone-lined pit for an oven. When the water holes dried up they moved, first to Nausgomab and later to a shelter they called Baboons' Den.

They had handed their rifles to the police at the start of the war, so they hunted with what they had left and with traps, and they were good at it. The best scene in the book has Martin chased up a ledge by a feral bull, Otto hanging on to its tail and then its nose, until Korn brought it down. They seined a pool with a net of tamarisk branches and pulled out fourteen big carp, and salted them with tamarisk twigs, because tamarisks sweat salt.

It ended with illness. By 1942 Korn had beriberi from the diet, and on September 2 Martin drove him to Windhoek for treatment and went back into the desert alone with Otto. He was arrested soon after. Both men were charged with minor offences and fined, friends paid, and neither was interned. Before the war was over the government had put them to work looking for groundwater, which was what they had been doing all along.

The rest of their story is shorter and sadder. Korn died in 1946, when his car went off the road near Windhoek at night. Martin went on to find a reliable water supply for Windhoek, ran the territory's branch of the Geological Survey from 1947 to 1963, and finished his career as professor of geology at Göttingen. He died in 1998. The book came out in German in the 1950s, and in English in 1957, translated by Edward Fitzgerald.

## Can you still see their shelter?

Yes, and it is the best roadside stop in Namibia. The remains of one of their shelters lie inside Namib-Naukluft Park, a short walk from the gravel road that crosses the Kuiseb Pass between Solitaire and Walvis Bay. You will see the overhang, a few stones and the canyon below, which is the point: you can stand where they sat and understand in a minute why nobody found them.

Two honest notes. The turning has been signposted in the past, but travellers report that the sign is not always there, so ask your guide or driver to stop rather than trusting yourself to spot it. And there are signed lookouts over the canyon near the top of the pass, from which a long path leads down towards Carp Cliff. It is a walk for a cool morning, not a hot afternoon.

This is why the Kuiseb belongs in a luxury trip and not only in a history book. It is on the way. Almost everyone who goes to Sossusvlei and then on to the coast at Swakopmund or Walvis Bay drives this road, or flies over it. Most of them do not know what happened in the canyon below.

## When should you go?

April to September for the trip most people picture, and November to mid-December for the best value. Zannier gives days of 20 to 25°C from April to September, with desert nights that can drop well below freezing in midwinter, so pack for both. October to December averages about 25°C. From December to March the days run 25 to 34°C, with the occasional storm.

The rule I use: book the cool season if dune walks and long days in the field are the reason for the trip, and book November if you want the same lodges at a lower rate and do not mind heat at midday. &Beyond's published 2026 rate drops from NAD 36,750 per person in high season to NAD 21,950 from November 1 to December 20, and Little Kulala's from US$1,653 to US$1,041. The Namibian dollar is pegged one to one to the South African rand.

## How do you get to Sossusvlei, and how many nights do you need?

Fly if you can, and give it three nights. Sesriem, the gate to the dunes, is 334 km from Windhoek, much of it on gravel, and Sossusvlei is a further 60 km inside the park. By road that is a long day each way. Most of the lodges below have their own airstrips, and the light-aircraft flight from Windhoek takes about an hour.

Three nights is the right number because the best of the Namib happens at the two ends of the day. You want one morning at Sossusvlei and Deadvlei, one at your lodge's own dunes or on a horse or in a balloon, and one free. Two nights works, but it makes every morning an early one.

Foreign adults pay NAD 280 a day to enter Namib-Naukluft Park from April 2026, children aged 9 to 15 pay NAD 180, and children of eight and under go free. Some lodge rates include park fees and some do not, so check before you compare prices.

## Do you need to be inside the park gate?

Only if you are not paying for a private one. The light at Sossusvlei is best in the first hour after sunrise, and the main gate at Sesriem opens around sunrise. Guests staying inside the park, at the state-run Sossus Dune Lodge, can reach the dunes before sunrise and stay after sunset. Sossus Dune Lodge has 25 chalets and is a simple, comfortable place to sleep, not a luxury lodge, but for photographers it is a real advantage.

The other way in early is Little Kulala, which has its own private gate into the park. Everywhere else, your guide will leave in the dark and join the queue.

## Where should you stay?

These are the five places I would build a Namib trip around, plus one on the coast if you want to keep going. A note on ratings: Forbes Travel Guide does not cover these lodges, so the useful mark here is the MICHELIN Key, and only one of the five in the dunes holds one.

### The grand one: Zannier Sonop

<figure><img src="https://www.zannierhotels.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Zannier-Sonop-Bedroom-04-@tibodhermy-for-Zannier-Hotels-scaled.jpg" alt="A tented bedroom at Zannier Sonop in the Namib Desert, with a four-poster bed and views over the desert." loading="lazy"><figcaption>A tent at Zannier Sonop. Image courtesy of <a href="https://www.zannierhotels.com/hotels/sonop/accommodation/two-bedroom-tents/" rel="noopener">Zannier Hotels</a>.</figcaption></figure>

**Zannier Sonop** is the only hotel in Namibia with Three MICHELIN Keys, kept in the 2026 selection, and it is the most complete desert hotel in the country. It is ten tents, eight One-Bedroom and two Two-Bedroom, raised on platforms on top of a granite boulder outcrop on the edge of the NamibRand Nature Reserve, furnished like a 1920s British expedition with four-poster beds, a bathtub facing the view and a telescope on every terrace. What nobody else has is the production: stables, with a 30-minute desert ride included, an open-air cinema on a 4.5-metre screen every second night, five-course dinners served by butlers in black tie, and a cigar lounge with a billiard table. There is a heated infinity pool and a spa. It has its own airstrip and is about an hour and twenty minutes by air from Windhoek. All tents are the same category, so ask for a One-Bedroom Tent; the Two-Bedroom Tents are for families, and children under six are not accepted. Zannier's own rate is from NAD 21,866 per tent per night all-inclusive, or NAD 16,407 full board, and the all-inclusive rate covers drinks other than Champagne and premium spirits, shared drives, e-bikes and the ride. The honest caveat is distance: Sonop's Sossusvlei day trip leaves at 4:30 in the morning and takes nine and a half hours. Breakfast on top of the Sonop dune is NAD 4,600 per person, and a helicopter to Deadvlei and back is NAD 47,250 per person. For a first trip to the Namib, if the lodge is as much the point as the dunes, nothing else comes close.

### For the stars: &Beyond Sossusvlei Desert Lodge

<figure><img src="https://www.andbeyond.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/Image-3-1.jpg" alt="Aerial view of &Beyond Sossusvlei Desert Lodge in its private reserve, with the Namib dunes and mountains behind." loading="lazy"><figcaption>&Beyond Sossusvlei Desert Lodge. Image courtesy of <a href="https://www.andbeyond.com/lodge-editorial/sossusvlei-desert-lodge/" rel="noopener">&Beyond</a>.</figcaption></figure>

**&Beyond Sossusvlei Desert Lodge** is the lodge for a couple who want to lie in bed and look at the sky. It sits in &Beyond's own 12,715-hectare reserve, which borders both Namib-Naukluft Park and the NamibRand Nature Reserve, Africa's first International Dark Sky Reserve, and it has ten Suites of 130 square metres in stone, glass and steel, plus the two-bedroom Star Dune Suite of 267 square metres for families. Every suite has a private plunge pool, a fireplace, an outdoor shower and, above the bed, a skylight that opens. The lodge has its own observatory with a Celestron telescope, and an astronomer visits on some nights, so ask before you book if that matters. Days go to the Big Dune Walk on the reserve's own star dunes, e-bike routes and, once per stay, a guided morning at Sossusvlei and Deadvlei with the climb up Big Daddy. The food is cooked in an open kitchen where you can join the chefs, with a walk-in wine cellar and a bar built from slabs of local granite. Rates for 2026 are NAD 21,950 per person per night sharing from November 1 to December 20, and NAD 36,750 from June to October; for 2027 they are NAD 22,830 and NAD 38,220. That includes meals, house wines and local spirits, laundry and airstrip transfers, but not park fees, helicopter or balloon flights. It is unrated by MICHELIN. For stargazing, it is the one.

### For the dunes at dawn: Wilderness Little Kulala

<figure><img src="https://www.wildernessdestinations.com/media/3gdlqgnn/wilderness-little-kulala-at-sunrise.jpg" alt="Wilderness Little Kulala at sunrise on the Kulala Wilderness Reserve, with the Namib dunes on the horizon." loading="lazy"><figcaption>Little Kulala at sunrise. Image courtesy of <a href="https://www.wildernessdestinations.com/africa/namibia/sossusvlei/little-kulala" rel="noopener">Wilderness</a>.</figcaption></figure>

**Wilderness Little Kulala** has the one thing the others cannot buy: its own private gate into the park. It sits on the 27,000-hectare Kulala Wilderness Reserve, which borders Namib-Naukluft, and its guides take you through it to Sossusvlei and Deadvlei, which is how you are on the dunes while everyone else is still at the main gate. There are eleven chalets for 22 guests, each with a private plunge pool, a rooftop deck for sundowners and a "Star Bed" for sleeping outside, plus one family unit. The camp was rebuilt and reopened in late 2020, and it now runs on solar power and has a spa. The kitchen cooks with Namibian produce, from dates and eembe fruit to venison and wild spinach, and serves dinners in the underground wine cellar. Rates are US$1,041 per person per night from November 1 to December 19, 2026, US$1,136 from January to March 2027 and US$1,868 in the 2027 high season from June to October, and unlike most of the others they include park fees as well as meals, local drinks and two activities a day. It holds no MICHELIN Key. If Sossusvlei is the reason you came, this is where I would put you.

### For the purist: Wolwedans

<figure><img src="https://www.wolwedans.com/wp-content/uploads/WOLWEDANS-COLLECTION-Landscapes-Daniel-Fowler-3-scaled.jpg" alt="Red dunes and grass plains of the NamibRand Nature Reserve near Wolwedans Dune Camp in Namibia." loading="lazy"><figcaption>The NamibRand Nature Reserve at Wolwedans. Photo: Daniel Fowler / <a href="https://www.wolwedans.com/dune-camp/" rel="noopener">Wolwedans</a>.</figcaption></figure>

**Wolwedans** is the one for travellers who want the desert more than the dunes everyone photographs. The Brückner family founded it in 1995 inside the NamibRand Nature Reserve, 220,000 hectares of private desert run for conservation, and it is now a small collection of camps. The original is Dune Camp, on top of a 250-metre dune: six tented rooms and one two-room Family Suite for no more than 16 guests, each with a Starbed on the deck and a plunge silo they call the Wolwedans Chiller. It runs on solar power and has no air conditioning, and it does not need it. For a couple who want privacy, the Mountain View Suite is exclusive use, adults only, with its own chef, guide, vehicle and plunge pool. The rate includes meals, house drinks, game drives and a full-day scenic safari, and the collection's village gardens supply the kitchen. Rates for 2026 are NAD 14,900 per person per night sharing at Dune Camp and NAD 20,950 at the Mountain View Suite, plus a NAD 1,600 sustainability levy per adult per night, with a minimum stay of two nights. The honest caveat: Sesriem is about two hours away by road, so Wolwedans is not where you stay for a Sossusvlei sunrise. It is where you stay to see the Namib empty.

### The newest: Luna Namib Collection

**Luna Namib Collection** opened on July 1, 2026, and it is the most ambitious thing the Namibian lodge group Gondwana has built. It is three standalone villas about 70 km south of Sesriem off the C27, each with a plunge pool, a private bar, a telescope and its own host, guide and chef. Muse and Solo sleep two; Opal has two bedrooms for a family. The concept is built around the phases of the moon, there is no mobile signal, and the price reflects the privacy: NAD 50,000 per villa per night for Muse or Solo and NAD 65,000 for Opal, fully inclusive. Stay two nights and a Sossusvlei excursion with a Champagne brunch is included, and from three nights the balloon and a helicopter flight are too. It is too new to have a rating or a track record. For a honeymoon, or for anyone who wants the whole desert to themselves, I would look at it seriously.

### If you continue to the coast: Shipwreck Lodge

**Shipwreck Lodge** is a different trip, far to the north on the Skeleton Coast, but it is the natural second half of a Namib journey and it holds Two MICHELIN Keys. It is the only lodge inside Skeleton Coast National Park, near Möwe Bay: ten cabins built to look like shipwrecks, each with a wood-burning stove, eight for couples and two for families. The days go to the clay castles of the Hoarusib River, shoreline drives, sandboarding and, with luck, desert elephants and brown hyena. Natural Selection, which runs it, publishes rates from NAD 17,995 to NAD 30,195 a night; let our advisors quote the exact rate for your dates. It is the place to go if the Namib has made you want more of it.

## A five-night plan, from the dunes to the sea

**Nights one to three, Sossusvlei.** Fly from Windhoek to Little Kulala or Sonop. The first morning, Sossusvlei and Deadvlei at first light. The second, the lodge's own desert: a horse at Sonop, a balloon, or a walk on the dunes. The third, nothing at all.

**Day four, the Kuiseb.** Drive or be driven north on the gravel road through Solitaire and over the Kuiseb Pass, and stop at the canyon. Walk to the shelter, look down at the riverbed, and read the first chapter of The Sheltering Desert while you are there. Continue to the coast at Walvis Bay or Swakopmund for the night.

**Night five, the coast or home.** Continue north to Shipwreck Lodge for the Skeleton Coast, or head home via Walvis Bay.

## The desert that kept them

What stays with me about Martin and Korn is not the hiding, which was a sensible decision by two men who did not want to be locked up. It is how much they liked it. They had a dog, a radio, a canyon full of game and a sky full of stars, and Martin wrote the book in a way that makes you want to go and find the overhang yourself. You can. It is a short walk from the road, and once you are standing in it, the canyon makes the rest of the story obvious.
