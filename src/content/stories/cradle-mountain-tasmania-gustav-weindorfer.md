Early in January 1910, three people climbed out of the button grass of Cradle Valley in north-west Tasmania and climbed Cradle Mountain. One was Ronald Smith, who would later be a major. One was Kate Weindorfer, a Tasmanian botanist. The third was her husband Gustav, an Austrian naturalist with a farm at Kindred near Devonport and a habit of naming things. Somewhere on that climb, as Smith told it later, Gustav stopped and said the sentence the park still quotes on its own website: "This must be a National Park for the people for all time. It is magnificent, and people must know about it and enjoy it."

He was not the first up. The University of Tasmania's Companion to Tasmanian History credits the surveyor Henry Hellyer with the first ascent, in 1831. What makes Weindorfer different is what he did next. He bought land below the mountain, built a chalet out of the forest around it, carried the bath in himself, and spent the rest of his life hosting walkers, writing to newspapers and badgering the government until the land between Cradle Mountain and Lake St Clair was protected. He got his reserve in 1922. He died ten years later, beside his motorcycle, on the track he had built.

The honest answer on Cradle Mountain is that most people see it from the car park at Dove Lake in an afternoon and leave, and that it rewards the opposite. The mountain is the start of the Overland Track, the walk Weindorfer's reserve made possible, and the best way to understand what he was arguing for is to walk away from it for six days. Here is his story, and how I would follow it now.

## Who was Gustav Weindorfer?

A clerk who wanted to be outdoors. He was born on 23 February 1874 at Spittal an der Drau in Carinthia, the son of a district governor, studied at an agricultural school at Mödling near Vienna, did his military service, worked in the wine trade, and in 1900 emigrated to Melbourne. There he took an office job, became honorary chancellor at the Austro-Hungarian consulate in 1901, and in the same year joined the Field Naturalists' Club of Victoria. He was, by the Australian Dictionary of Biography's description, a mountaineer and an amateur botanist, who organised botanising camps in the Victorian Alps and sent Australian seeds home to the natural history museum in Vienna.

At the club he met Kate Cowle, a fellow member who had recently moved to Melbourne from Tasmania. They married on 1 February 1906 at Stowport, Tasmania, and spent a good part of the honeymoon camped on Mount Roland collecting plants, which tells you most of what you need to know about the marriage. They settled on a farm at Kindred, near Devonport, and he turned out to be an excellent farmer.

## What did he find at Cradle Mountain?

A botanist's paradise, in his own phrase "a veritable Eldorado for the botanist". His first trip was in January 1909, with Dr Charles Sutton and a local guide, and fog turned them back below the summit. The second, a year later with Kate and Smith, got to the top. It is the second trip that produced the sentence, and the plan: a chalet in the valley below, where walkers and climbers could stay, and a road and a park to bring them.

Cradle Valley in 1910 had no shelter for a visitor, and the nearest point a horse and cart could reach was about eight miles away. Kate bought land there with her inheritance from her father, and in 1912 Gustav started building.

## What was Waldheim?

A chalet he built with his own hands from King Billy pine cut on the site, and named Waldheim, home in the forest. The first rooms were ready by Christmas 1912. Because no cart could reach the valley, he carried in what the house needed himself, including the baths and the stoves. Over the next years he enlarged it, cut and marked tracks, named the lakes and lookouts around it, and wrote articles for the Launceston papers and the Victorian Naturalist arguing for a road and a park.

He was also, by every account, a very good host. The Australian Dictionary of Biography calls him courtly and jovial, a man who often sang to his guests and had a mischievous sense of humour. Guests walked in for their stay, and one local history records them remembering the Weindorfers' humour and generous serves of wombat stew. Carved on the wall of the chalet was a motto: "This is Waldheim, where there is no time and nothing matters."

## What happened after Kate died?

He became the hermit of Cradle Mountain, which is not quite what he wanted. Kate died in April 1916, after an illness, and was buried at the Don, near Devonport. The war made things worse. Weindorfer had become an Australian citizen, was a justice of the peace and had tried to join the Australian Imperial Force, but an Austrian on a remote mountain was an easy target, and some locals spread rumours that he was a spy. In 1917 he sold the farm at Kindred and moved up to Waldheim for good, running it single-handed, acting as ranger and keeping the official weather records.

He kept campaigning, travelling and lecturing for the park, and in 1922 some 158,000 acres stretching from Cradle Mountain to Lake St Clair were proclaimed a scenic reserve and wildlife sanctuary. It is the core of today's Cradle Mountain-Lake St Clair National Park, and since 1982 part of the Tasmanian Wilderness World Heritage Area, which covers about a fifth of Tasmania.

In April 1931 he bought a motorcycle. On 5 May 1932, trying to start it on the track he had built to link Waldheim to the plains, he died of heart failure. He was found the next day beside the motorcycle and its sidecar, near what is now the Ronny Creek car park, where the Overland Track begins. He was buried in the valley, under a stone cairn. Waldheim kept taking guests until 1974; in 1976 it was demolished and rebuilt as a faithful replica.

The last chapter happened this year. Kate's remains were moved from the Don Congregational Cemetery and laid beside Gustav's at Waldheim, more than 110 years after her death, and a stone plaque recognising her as an equal partner in the vision was unveiled in May 2026. It had taken a long time for the park to give her the credit, and it is the first thing I would walk to.

## How do you follow him today?

Start where he did, in Cradle Valley. The road to Dove Lake is closed to private cars while the park shuttle runs, so leave the car at the visitor centre and take the bus. It stops at Ronny Creek, where the Overland Track starts and where Weindorfer was found, and Waldheim is a short, easy walk from there through myrtle and King Billy pine. The chalet is open, with a small display inside, and Gustav and Kate are beside it.

Then do the two day walks. The Dove Lake circuit is about six kilometres, two to three hours, under the mountain the whole way, with the old boat shed in every photograph. The summit is the other: 12.8 kilometres return, about eight hours and 600 metres of climbing, with real boulder scrambling at the top. It is the climb the Weindorfers made, and it is weather-dependent in every month. Discover Tasmania warns that snow can fall even in summer.

Then walk the Overland Track, which is the thing his reserve made. It runs 65 kilometres from Ronny Creek to Lake St Clair, with a side trail up Mount Ossa, at 1,617 metres the highest peak in Tasmania, and most people take six days. It is north to south only. From 1 October to 31 May you must book it with Tasmania Parks and Wildlife Service, who allow 34 independent walkers to start each day and charge A$312 per adult. The walk ends at Narcissus Hut, where a ferry crosses Lake St Clair to the visitor centre, or you walk the last 17.5 kilometres along the shore.

## Which way should you walk it?

Guided, on private huts, unless you love carrying a tent. The **Tasmanian Walking Company** runs the Cradle Mountain Signature Walk, six nights on the track in its own private huts, each with six twin rooms, hot showers and a drying room, plus a standing camp at Pine Forest Moor. Groups are six to twelve, with two guides. The six-night walk runs daily from 1 October to 1 May and starts from A$4,795 per person twin share, with a five-day version in spring and autumn from A$3,595 and a new seven-day Adventure Walk this summer from A$5,495. For most people I put on the Overland Track, this is the right answer.

## Where should you stay before and after?

At the end of the track, **Pumphouse Point**. It is a five-storey pumphouse the Hydro built out in Lake St Clair, finished in 1940 and never needed, reached by a 250-metre concrete flume, and since January 2015 a hotel: 18 rooms between the Pumphouse on the water and the Shorehouse on the bank, with the lounge and dining room in the Shorehouse. Meals, the larder, e-bikes and rowing boats are included, and the Pumphouse rooms, from about A$750 a night, are the ones to ask for. In 2025 it added the Retreats, two free-standing suites on the lake edge with drinks included. Two nights here after six on the track is the best reward in Tasmania.

At the start, **Cradle Mountain Lodge**, a Peppers property at the edge of the park, is the practical choice: 86 cabins and suites, the Waldheim Alpine Spa with its sauna, steam room and hot tub, and the park entrance close by. Ask for one of the refurbished King Billy Suites. Let our advisors quote current rates for both.

Getting there is the easy part. Fly to Launceston and drive about two to two and a half hours, or fly from Melbourne to Devonport, which is 86 kilometres away. Buy a Tasmanian Holiday Pass online before you go, A$95.50 per vehicle for up to two months, and a shuttle ticket, A$15.50 for 72 hours.

## No time and nothing matters

Weindorfer spent twenty years telling people that this valley should belong to everyone, and most of them did not listen until he had done the work himself: the chalet, the tracks, the names, the lectures, the weather records. The park is the result, and the Overland Track is the proof that he was right about who it was for. Walk out of Cradle Valley on the first morning past the cairn where he and Kate lie, and by the time you reach the lake at the other end you will understand the motto on the wall.
