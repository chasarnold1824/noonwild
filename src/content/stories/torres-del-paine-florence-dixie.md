On the 11th of December 1878, a party of four men and one woman sailed from Liverpool on the steamer *Britannia*, bound for the Strait of Magellan. The woman was Lady Florence Dixie, a daughter of the Marquess of Queensberry, a mother of two who had given birth to her second son that same year, and she had organized the whole thing. Her reason, as she later explained it, was refreshingly free of science or empire. She wanted to go somewhere nobody she knew had been. "Precisely because it was an outlandish place and so far away," she wrote, "I chose it."

A few weeks into the ride north from Punta Arenas, which the English then called Sandy Point, she looked up from the pampa and saw three reddish peaks standing above everything else. She thought they were, in shape, exact copies of Cleopatra's Needle, the Egyptian obelisk, and that is what she called them. We call them the Torres del Paine.

She was not the first person to see them. The Aonikenk, or Tehuelche, had named the whole range Paine long before, from their word for blue. But she was, as far as anyone can tell, the first person to go there for the pleasure of it, and her book, *Across Patagonia*, published in 1880, is one of the first descriptions of the towers that most of the world ever read. Which is why the park's own lodges now call her its first tourist.

The honest answer on Torres del Paine is that the towers are as good as she said, the wind is worse than you expect, and the park rewards the traveler who gives it time. Here is her story, and how I would follow it now.

## Who was Lady Florence Dixie?

A Scottish aristocrat who seems to have treated every convention of her class as a suggestion. She was born on 24 May 1855 at Cummertrees in Dumfriesshire, a twin to Lord James Douglas, and at nineteen she married Sir Alexander Beaumont Churchill Dixie, known to everyone as Beau. Her party for Patagonia was her husband, her twin, her brother the Marquess of Queensberry, and a friend, Julius Beerbohm, whose own book on Patagonia had just come out and who drew the sketches for hers. She was the only woman.

Patagonia was the beginning rather than the high point. In 1881 the *Morning Post* sent her to South Africa as a field correspondent, the first woman it appointed, to cover the First Boer War and the aftermath of the Anglo-Zulu War. She interviewed the Zulu king Cetshwayo while the British were holding him, and her reporting and books on Zululand helped make the case for his brief restoration in 1883. She argued for votes for women, for equality in marriage and divorce and for dress women could move in, and in 1895 she became the first president of the British Ladies' Football Club. She died at her home at Glen Stuart in Dumfriesshire on 7 November 1905, at fifty.

## What did she actually find in 1879?

The towers, a lake and the first good look at the Andes that her party had earned. The ride from the Strait took them past Cape Gregorio, north to Laguna Blanca and across the Gallegos River, living largely on guanaco and ostrich meat, with a prairie fire, an earthquake and a night when a wild stallion came into camp to fight for their horses. Then, with a few companions and a guide, she pushed on through forest, bog and river to the edge of the Paine.

What she found there she described in the book's best pages. A lake whose waters, she wrote, were "of the most extraordinarily brilliant blue I have ever beheld," with white glaciers and the clouds above them mirrored on its surface. Las Torres Patagonia, the estate at the foot of the towers, identifies it as Laguna Azul, in what is now the park's eastern sector. Behind it, from a hill, she looked out over what she called "a forest of white peaks." One of Beerbohm's plates carries a caption that sums up how the party felt about it: "We were the first who ever burst on to that silent sea."

She got one thing wrong, and it is a nice one. She was told the Needles were the remains of the crater of an extinct volcano. They are not. The towers are granite, from a body of magma that pushed up between older, darker layers of sedimentary rock about 12.5 million years ago and cooled underground; ice then quarried away almost everything around it. The dark rock you see capping the Cuernos del Paine is what is left of the roof.

## Did she really tell Darwin he was wrong?

Yes, politely, and in writing. On 29 October 1880, from Glen Stuart, she wrote to Charles Darwin about the tuco-tuco, a burrowing rodent he had described in his account of the *Beagle* voyage. Darwin had written that the animal never came to the surface and that blindness was common among them. Dixie had watched several out of their burrows on moonlit nights in Patagonia and two in daylight, and had caught one that could see perfectly well. She was sure, she told him, it would interest him to know "that tho' this may be the usual habits of the tucutuco that there are exceptions."

The editors of Darwin's letters think they were both right: Darwin had studied one species near Maldonado in Uruguay, and she had almost certainly seen another. Darwin replied. Her next letter, on 4 November, mentioned in passing that a jaguar family had "attacked me & followed me up a tree," that she had shot the mother, and that she had saved a cub. She took the cub home and called him Affums. He lived with the Dixies at The Fishery, their house on the Thames near Windsor, until he attacked several deer in Windsor Great Park and was sent to London Zoo. On 29 November she sent Darwin a copy of *Across Patagonia*. After his death she wrote to his son Francis to ask permission to dedicate her next book to him.

That letter is the reason I like her. She went to Patagonia for the adventure, shot the game she ate, and still came home and wrote to the most famous naturalist alive with a field note he did not have.

## When should you go?

October to April, and choose your month by what you can tolerate. The park is best from spring to early autumn. November to January brings the longest days and the strongest wind, which is the part people underestimate. March and April are the wettest months. In winter CONAF, the park authority, closes the W circuit and the boat crossings on lakes Pehoé and Grey, and requires an authorized guide for harder routes.

You need a park ticket, and you cannot buy it at the gate. Since 1 May 2026 CONAF sells entries only online and in advance at pasesparques.cl, as a single-day pass or a multi-day pass valid for up to ten consecutive days. If you are staying inside or beside the park for four nights, buy the multi-day pass.

## How do you follow her route today?

Fly to Punta Arenas, where she landed, or to Puerto Natales, and drive north. The park is about 112 kilometres from Puerto Natales, roughly two hours by road, and more than 300 kilometres from Punta Arenas, closer to five. That is why my rule is four nights at the minimum: the first and last days go mostly to the road.

Then spend a day on her side of the park. The eastern sector, entered by the Laguna Amarga gate, is where the lake she described lies: Laguna Azul. Las Torres runs a guided outing there that tells her story along the way, and any good private guide will take you. Go in the early morning, when the wind is lowest and the lake is most likely to hold the reflection she described.

The walk to the base of the towers, the Mirador Base Torres, is the one everyone does, and it should be. CONAF is not regulating access to that trail for the 2026 season, but start early and carry layers for the wind.

## Where should you stay?

Close to her side of the park, if the eastern sector is the point of the trip. **Awasi Patagonia** sits on a private reserve above Lake Sarmiento, about 25 minutes from the Laguna Amarga gate, and holds three MICHELIN Keys, the guide's highest distinction. It is a collection of separate villas of about 90 square metres (970 square feet), each with a wood-burning fireplace and an outdoor hot tub facing the park, and each assigned its own private guide and 4x4 for the whole stay, so the day is planned around you rather than a group schedule. Meals, drinks, excursions and transfers from Puerto Natales are included. It is the place I would put anyone who wants to follow a story like Dixie's rather than a fixed menu of outings.

If you would rather wake up inside the park, **explora Patagonia** stands on the shore of Lake Pehoé at the Salto Chico waterfall, facing the Paine massif. It has 49 rooms, six of them suites, and the Explorer Suites are the ones to ask for, for the view of the massif. Its spa, 100 metres from the main building, has a heated covered pool, a sauna and four open-air hot tubs above the Paine River. Rates are all-inclusive, with guided excursions in small groups. It is the better choice for walkers who want to be inside the park rather than beside it, and it publishes its own account of Dixie's ride.

## The happy time

Dixie went home with a dog called Pucho, who had followed her down the pier at Sandy Point and onto the steamer, a jaguar cub and a book. The rest of her life was spent on causes that made her a great many enemies, and she seems to have enjoyed most of those too. But she never wrote about anything quite the way she wrote about those months on horseback. "Taking it all in all," she ended the book, "it was a very happy time, and a time on whose like I would gladly look again." Stand at Laguna Azul on a still morning with the Needles in the water and you will see what she meant.
