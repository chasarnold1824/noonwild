In 1941, when the British took Ethiopia from Mussolini, a young Italian colonial official called Felice Benuzzi was among the men they captured. He had been born in Vienna in 1910, grown up in Trieste and joined the Italian Colonial Service in 1938, and he was a keen climber with nothing left to climb. The British sent him south to Prisoner of War Camp 354, near the small town of Nanyuki in the middle of Kenya. Most days the camp sat under cloud. Then one morning the cloud lifted, and above the wire, filling half the sky, was Mount Kenya.

He could not stop looking at it. Over the next two years Benuzzi decided he was going to climb it, and then come back. Not to escape the war, which would have been hopeless from the middle of Kenya, but to escape the boredom of the camp, which was killing everyone in it slowly. He recruited a doctor, Giovanni Balletto, who knew mountains, and Vincenzo Barsotti, who had never climbed one in his life. They had no map. What they had was the label on a tin of meat, which carried a drawing of the mountain.

The honest answer to why you should go is that this is one of the best places in Africa to be on safari, and almost nobody who flies over Nanyuki knows what happened under them. The plateau north and west of the mountain, Laikipia, is old cattle country turned into private conservancies, with black rhino and lodges as good as anything in East Africa, and Mount Kenya stands over all of it. Here is the story, how I would plan the trip, and where I would put you.

## What did the prisoners actually do?

They built their own kit, broke out of the camp, climbed for the summit of Africa's second highest mountain, missed it, climbed another peak instead, and walked back in. It took eighteen days.

The preparation took months. Two hammers stolen from the camp workshop became ice axes. Crampons were hammered out of scrap metal from the rubbish heap. The netting of a bed was unpicked and twisted into a rope, which UKClimbing's review of the book calls, fairly, totally inadequate. They saved food, made rucksacks and clothes, and on January 24, 1943, got themselves out of the camp and into the forest. They left a note behind saying they would be back.

Their target was Batian, the main summit, at 5,199 metres. They went up through bamboo and forest into the moorland, made a base camp high in the mountain's northern valleys, and Benuzzi and Balletto set out for the northwest ridge. A storm turned them back high on the mountain. So they did the next best thing. A few days later they climbed Point Lenana, the third summit, at about 4,985 metres, and planted a homemade Italian flag. They left their names in a bottle in the summit cairn.

Then they went home, which was the camp. They gave themselves up, and were each sentenced to 28 days in the cells. The commandant cut it to seven, in recognition, Benuzzi later wrote, of their "sporting effort". A few days afterwards a party of British climbers reached the top of Lenana and found the flag.

## What happened to Benuzzi afterwards?

He wrote the book, and then he became an ambassador. The Italian edition, Fuga sul Kenya, came first, and the English version, No Picnic on Mount Kenya, was published in London in 1952. It was reissued in 2015, and it is the best mountain book I know about people who were not supposed to be on the mountain at all. It was made into a film, The Ascent, in 1994.

Benuzzi went home to Italy in 1946, joined the diplomatic service two years later, and served in Paris, Brisbane, Karachi, Canberra and Berlin before becoming Italy's ambassador to Uruguay in 1973. In retirement he led Italy's delegation on Antarctica. He died in Rome in 1988. The col between Point Dutton and the Petit Gendarme, high on the northwest side of the mountain, is named Benuzzi Col after him.

What I like about the story is that nobody comes out of it badly. The prisoners kept their word, and the commandant kept his sense of humour.

## Can you still see the mountain they climbed?

Yes, and you will see it more often than they did. On a clear morning Mount Kenya fills the view from Arijiju, Segera and the Fairmont, and from Nanyuki itself, whose airstrip is the closest to the national park. The mountain is a national park, established in 1949 and a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1997.

You can also climb to where they stood. Point Lenana is a trek, not a technical climb, and it is the summit most people reach. Batian and Nelion, the two higher peaks, are rock and ice climbs that need a rope and a real mountaineer. Kenya Wildlife Service runs three official routes: Sirimon, off the Nanyuki to Meru road, which is known for its wildlife; Naro Moru, the shortest and steepest; and Chogoria, the long, beautiful one, where you carry tents. Rough Guides is firm that you should not try to reach Lenana in under 72 hours from the bottom, and recommends five or six days, so plan a week with the descent.

Two practical rules. You must go with a certified guide, which Kenya Wildlife Service requires of every visitor. And non-resident adults pay US$70 a day to enter the park, children US$35, payable online through eCitizen. For most travelers I would not make the climb the trip. I would make Laikipia the trip, and add the mountain if someone in the group has been wanting it for years.

## When should you go?

January to February, or July to September. Those are Kenya's dry seasons, the grass is shorter, the animals come to the water and the mountain clears more often. Rough Guides names February and August as the most reliable months on the mountain itself, with January and most of July usually fine too.

The long rains arrive around April and May, and some Laikipia camps close then. November brings the short rains, which are lighter, but this is the month when I would check that a camp is open before falling in love with it.

## How do you get there, and how many nights do you need?

Fly, and give it four nights at least. Light aircraft leave Nairobi for Nanyuki and for the Lewa airstrip, about fifty minutes to Lewa. By road, the Fairmont Mount Kenya Safari Club is about three hours from Jomo Kenyatta International Airport. If you are combining Laikipia with the Masai Mara, which most first trips to Kenya do, Laikipia is the better place to begin: it is high, cool and quiet, and it is where you see rhino.

Four nights is the right number because Laikipia is not a game-drive-and-done safari. The conservancies let you walk, ride horses, take a camel out, fly a helicopter up to the mountain lakes and track rhino on foot with the rangers who protect them. You want time for two of those.

## Where should you stay?

These are the four places I would build a Mount Kenya trip around. Three are on private conservancies on the Laikipia plateau and the fourth sits right under the mountain at Nanyuki. They are very different, so read for the kind of trip you want, not the most famous name.

### The private house: Arijiju

**Arijiju** is the most beautiful private house on the plateau, and it is taken by one group at a time. It sits on the Borana Conservancy, former cattle ranch land now run for black rhino, and it was finished in 2016 to a design by the London studio Michaelis Boyd. It is built into a low slope facing south, so every bedroom and the main living rooms look straight at Mount Kenya, with grass on the roofs, local stone and timber, solar power and a staircase cut into a rock outcrop at the entrance. There are five suites: three in the main house around a courtyard garden, and two in separate guest houses. What it has that the others do not is the sense of being someone's home, with a library, a cinema, a games room, a clay tennis court and a sprung-floor squash court kept out of sight, and a spa with a vaulted hammam, a wood-burning hot tub and a plunge pool, beside a cantilevered swimming pool. Days go to game drives and walks, horse riding, mountain biking and rhino tracking on Borana, and, for an extra charge, a helicopter up to fly-fish in the lakes of Mount Kenya. Meals are served wherever you like, on the terrace above the waterhole, by the pool or out in the bush. Agency rates for 2026 to 2027 run from about US$2,667 to US$3,467 per person per night sharing, fully inclusive of meals and selected drinks. For a family, or two couples who want the mountain to themselves, nothing in Kenya does it better.

### The art lover's reserve: Segera

**Segera** is Jochen Zeitz's 50,000-acre reserve on the Laikipia plateau, and it is the most ambitious place to stay in the region. The land was overgrazed ranch when Zeitz took it on, and it has been restored for wildlife, around a philosophy his foundation calls the four Cs: conservation, community, culture and commerce. There are six timber and thatch Garden Villas raised above the grass, each with a private deck looking out towards Mount Kenya, plus three houses for families and groups: Segera House, Villa Segera, on stilts with its own pool, and The Farmhouse, the original ranch homestead. Thirty guests is the maximum. What nobody else has is the art. Pieces from the Zeitz Collection of contemporary African art hang in the Stables, which doubles as bar and gallery, and stand in the sculpture gardens. Dinner can be in the Wine Tower, or on the terraces of the Paddock House facing the mountain. For one night, ask for the Nay Palad Bird Nest, an elevated sleep-out for two with a bed on the roof and a 360-degree view. There is a spa with a rasul steam chamber, and a private airstrip a few minutes away, about 45 minutes by air from Nairobi. Let our advisors quote current rates. For a couple who care as much about what is on the walls as what is in the grass, this is where I would put you.

### The family-run camp: Sirikoi

<figure><img src="https://www.andbeyond.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/55-pool_sunset.jpg" alt="The swimming pool at Sirikoi on the Lewa Wildlife Conservancy in Kenya at sunset." loading="lazy"><figcaption>The pool at Sirikoi at sunset. Image courtesy of <a href="https://www.andbeyond.com/places-to-stay/africa/kenya/sirikoi-camp-lewa-wildlife-conservancy/" rel="noopener">&Beyond</a>.</figcaption></figure>

**Sirikoi** is the Laikipia camp I would choose for a couple on a first safari in Kenya. It is small, warm and run by a family, and it sits on the Lewa Wildlife Conservancy, one of the great rhino sanctuaries of Africa, fifteen to twenty minutes' drive from the Lewa airstrip. Willie and Sue Roberts opened it in 2000, and the Roberts family still runs it. There are four tents, each with a private deck, a fireplace, a rain shower and a Victorian bath with a view through the gauze, plus the two-bedroom Sirikoi Cottage and the three-bedroom Sirikoi House, which comes with its own heated pool. Meals are on the main deck, with drinks and canapés around the fire before dinner, and much of what you eat comes from the camp's one-acre organic garden. The camp runs on solar power and spring water. The experiences that make it are Lewa's: day and night drives, guided walks, the conservancy's operations room and its tracker dogs, and, for extra, horses, camels, a helicopter and the waterfall pool in the Ngare Ndare Forest. Ask for a tent away from the main area: the two nearest the mess have the path running past the front. For a family, the Cottage or the House is the better buy. Children are welcome from six. Let our advisors quote current rates and check the season, because the camp closes for part of the rains. For Lewa, and for rhino, it is the one.

### The legend under the mountain: Fairmont Mount Kenya Safari Club

**The Fairmont Mount Kenya Safari Club** is where Laikipia's glamour started, and it is the closest of the four to the mountain and to the old camp. It began in the 1930s as a private house called Mawingo, became a hotel, and was bought in 1959 by the actor William Holden with Ray Ryan and Carl Hirschmann, who opened it as a club that June. Its guest list since has run from Winston Churchill to Bing Crosby, Conrad Hilton and Lyndon Johnson. It stands in the foothills just outside Nanyuki, a few miles from the equator, in about 100 acres of gardens with a golf course, a heated pool, a spa and an animal orphanage that shares the grounds with the Mount Kenya Wildlife Conservancy, where the critically endangered mountain bongo is bred to return to the forest. There are more than a hundred rooms, suites and cottages, and the ones to ask for are the William Holden Cottages, two-bedroom houses of about 95 square metres with Mount Kenya views, or, for a couple, the Manor Equatorial Suite, the only room set along the equator, with a balcony facing the mountain. The Colobus Grill is the signature restaurant, Tusks serves all day on a terrace looking at the peaks, and ZeBar has the panoramic view. It is 15 km from Nanyuki airstrip, about 30 minutes, and has its own strip for charters. It has been named Africa's Leading Hotel at the World Travel Awards several times, most recently in 2025. It is a hotel, not a safari camp, and it feels it: book it for the first and last nights of a Laikipia trip, or as the base for a climb. Let our advisors quote current rates.

## A six-night plan, from the mountain to the plateau

**Night one, Nanyuki.** Fly or drive from Nairobi to the Fairmont Mount Kenya Safari Club. Have a drink on the terrace at dusk, when the cloud usually lifts off the peaks. That is the view Benuzzi had over the wire.

**Nights two to four, a conservancy.** Move to Sirikoi on Lewa, or Arijiju on Borana if you have the group. Track rhino on foot, ride out at dawn, and fly up to the mountain lakes if the weather holds.

**Nights five and six, Segera.** Finish with the art, the Wine Tower and a night in the Bird Nest, then fly back to Nairobi, or on to the Mara.

If someone wants Lenana, replace the conservancy nights with a five or six day trek on the Sirimon route, and go back to the plateau afterwards to recover in a bath.

## The mountain that let them go

What stays with me about Benuzzi is not the escape, which was ridiculous, or even the climb, which was brave. It is that they went back. They had stood on top of Lenana with a homemade flag and the whole of Kenya below them, and then they walked down and handed themselves in, because they had said they would. The mountain is still there above Nanyuki, and on a clear morning from a Laikipia deck you can trace the ridge they tried. You do not have to climb it to understand them. You only have to look up.
