In the summer of 1928, Richard Halliburton asked the Panama Canal to treat him as a ship. He was 28, a Princeton graduate from Tennessee who had already made a career out of doing improbable things and writing them up, and he wanted to swim the canal from one ocean to the other, through the locks, the way a freighter would. The governor of the Canal Zone, M. L. Walker, said yes in writing.

So the canal did what it did with every ship. It measured him. Tolls were charged by tonnage, and Halliburton was weighed, entered in the books as the SS Halliburton and charged accordingly. The bill came to 36 cents. Nearly a century and a great many supertankers later, it is still the lowest toll anyone has paid to cross the Panama Canal.

He swam it over ten days in August 1928, about 50 hours in the water in all, with a rowboat alongside him carrying a marksman whose job was to watch for alligators. He was locked up and down like any vessel. Then he went home and put it in a book.

The honest answer on the Panama Canal today is that most people see it badly: an hour on a crowded terrace at Miraflores, a ship that may or may not arrive, and back to the hotel. It deserves a day on the water and two nights in the right places. Here is what Halliburton did, and how I would see the canal now.

## Who was Richard Halliburton?

The most popular travel writer in America between the wars, and very nearly forgotten now. He was born on January 9, 1900, in Brownsville, Tennessee, and grew up in Memphis. He graduated from Princeton in 1921, and instead of settling down he set off around the world, climbing the Matterhorn, swimming the Hellespont because Lord Byron had, and slipping into the reflecting pool at the Taj Mahal by moonlight after the gates had closed.

That trip became *The Royal Road to Romance*, published in 1925, which stayed a best-seller for years and was translated into 15 languages. The formula held for the rest of his life. He was not a scientist or a surveyor. He was a young man who did the thing everyone else only daydreamed about, at some personal risk, and then wrote about it with total delight. *The Glorious Adventure* followed in 1927. The Panama swim went into *New Worlds to Conquer*, in 1929.

Later he flew around the world in an open-cockpit biplane called the Flying Carpet with the pilot Moye Stephens, leaving on Christmas Day 1930 and coming back eighteen months later with *The Flying Carpet* (1932). He took an elephant from a Paris zoo over the Alps for *Seven League Boots* (1935). It is fair to say his stunts have aged better than some of his attitudes, but the curiosity was real, and it sent a generation of American readers abroad.

## How did he swim the canal?

Slowly, in stages, and with the full machinery of the canal working for 150 pounds or so of swimmer. The accounts put his weight at between 140 and 150 pounds. The canal charged him as a vessel under the ton rate, which is how a man came to pay 36 cents for the same passage as an ocean liner.

He started at the Atlantic end and swam toward the Pacific, a distance of about 50 miles, the length of the canal itself. Most of that is not a ditch at all but Gatun Lake, the reservoir created when the Chagres River was dammed, and the Culebra Cut, also called the Gaillard Cut, the narrow trench blasted through the continental divide. He swam a few hours a day for ten days. The escort followed in a rowboat: an oarsman, and a rifleman ready for the alligators that lived along the banks.

The locks were the part that made it a story. He was raised and lowered in them just as a ship would be, with the gates swung and the chambers filled and drained for a single swimmer.

The canal still tells the story on its own official channels, and the 36 cents is in Guinness World Records and on the Panama Canal Museum's list of canal facts.

## What does it cost to cross the canal now?

A great deal more than 36 cents, which is half the fun of telling the story at dinner. Large ships routinely pay hundreds of thousands of dollars in tolls, and the biggest container ships pay more than a million. When drought restrictions limited the number of daily transits in August 2026, the operator of a gas carrier paid US$5.3 million at auction just for a slot to cross, on top of its toll. Halliburton's record looks safe.

For a traveler, the price of crossing is the price of a boat ticket or a cruise, and that is where I would start.

## How should you see the canal today?

From the water first, and from the locks second. The visitor centers are worth an hour, but nothing replaces going through.

**A partial transit.** Day boats leave from Flamenco Marina on the Amador Causeway, or from Gamboa, and take you through the Pacific locks at Pedro Miguel and Miraflores, the three steps that carry ships between Gatun Lake and the Pacific. It takes about five to six hours, usually with lunch on board. The canal authority sets the direction and timing each day, so be relaxed about which way you are going. Several operators run only on Saturdays outside the busiest months, and more often from January to March, so book weeks ahead for a dry-season trip.

**A full transit.** Panama Marine Adventures, the operator of the Pacific Queen, runs occasional full transits of about twelve hours, from the Pacific through Miraflores and Pedro Miguel, along the Culebra Cut, across Gatun Lake past Barro Colorado Island and down through the Gatun Locks to the Caribbean. It is a long day, and it is the closest thing to Halliburton's route. Ask an advisor for dates, because they do not run every week.

**By ship.** A full daylight transit is a highlight of a small-ship cruise. Windstar's seven-night Costa Rica and Panama Canal voyage, from Colón to Puerto Caldera, crosses the canal on its second day, with sailings almost weekly from December to February. For something longer, Regent Seven Seas' Seven Seas Grandeur sails a 16-night Panama Canal transit from Los Angeles to Miami on January 24, 2027.

**From the locks.** The Miraflores Visitor Center, on the Pacific side and about half an hour from Casco Viejo, is open daily from 8 a.m. to 6 p.m. and costs US$17.22 for a non-resident adult. It has terraces and a large viewing deck over the locks, and a theater showing a 3D film on the canal, narrated by Morgan Freeman, included with entry. The canal authority is modernizing the center through the end of 2026, so expect queues; check the transit schedule on the official site and arrive when ships are due. On the Atlantic side, the Agua Clara Visitor Center (US$10) looks over the newer Neopanamax locks on one side and Gatun Lake on the other, and the ticket includes the Gatun Locks viewing deck, which opened in April 2025.

## When should you go?

In the dry season, December to April. Ships cross every day of the year, but from May to November afternoon rain is near certain, and October and November are the wettest months. Mid-December to February is the busiest and most expensive time; March and April are hotter, quieter and still dry, which is the trade I would make. Early December is a good shoulder month if you can live with the odd shower.

## Where should you stay?

**Sofitel Legend Casco Viejo** is the hotel I would choose for the city. It holds a MICHELIN Key and is Forbes Travel Guide Recommended, and it occupies the old Club Unión, built in 1917 on the water at the edge of the old town, a few minutes' walk from the Canal Museum. There are 159 rooms, most with sea views. The hotel's restaurant is Caleta, with AMMI on the rooftop for a drink over the bay. Ask for an Ocean Front Legendary Suite, about 95 square meters. Let our advisors quote current rates. For a first night in Panama, nothing else puts you so squarely in the city's history.

**Gamboa Rainforest Reserve**, about 16 miles from Panama City, is the one to add for the canal itself. It sits in Soberanía National Park above the Chagres River where it meets the canal and Gatun Lake, where the Culebra Cut, which Halliburton swam, opens onto the lake, and its aerial tram rises through the forest canopy to a tower with views over the water. It is comfortable rather than grand, but no other hotel puts you this close to the ships. Birders should look at **Canopy Tower** instead, nearby on Semaphore Hill: a 1965 radar tower turned into an all-inclusive lodge with a handful of rooms above the treetops.

A sensible shape for the trip is two nights in Casco Viejo with the partial transit on the day in between, then a night at Gamboa, and then the Pacific coast or the islands if you have more time.

## Thirty-six cents

Halliburton died the way he lived. In March 1939 he set out from Hong Kong for San Francisco aboard a Chinese junk called the Sea Dragon. The junk was lost in a typhoon west of Midway, and he was never found. He was 39.

What survives is the receipt. Every ship that pays its millions to cross the isthmus now is measured against the same tables that once charged a swimmer 36 cents, and the canal is proud enough of him to tell the story itself. You do not need to swim it. But go through the locks once, and watch those gates swing open for your little boat, and you will see why he wanted to.
