In the summer of 1816, a very large Italian stood on the west bank of the Nile at Thebes and looked at a colossal stone head lying in the ruins. Giovanni Battista Belzoni was six foot seven. A few years earlier he had been earning his living on the London stage as "the Patagonian Samson," a strongman at Sadler's Wells whose act, by one account, involved carrying up to a dozen smaller men on a steel frame. He had come to Egypt with his wife, Sarah, to sell the Pasha a water wheel. The Pasha did not buy it. So Belzoni took a job moving antiquities for the British consul, Henry Salt, and with ropes, rollers, poles and a team of local men he dragged the colossal bust of Ramesses II now known as the Younger Memnon from the Ramesseum to the river. It went on show at the British Museum, where it still is.

That was the warm-up. Later that year he sailed south into Nubia and found the Great Temple of Abu Simbel buried so deep in sand that only the tops of its giant statues showed. He came back the next summer, dug through the drift to the doorway, and at the start of August 1817 became the first person in modern times to walk inside. That October, in the Valley of the Kings, he found the tomb of Seti I: long, deep, and painted as if the artists had left the week before. A few months later he found the way into the second pyramid at Giza. He did all of this in about two years, as a former circus act whose only training was some study of hydraulics as a young man.

The honest part of the story is that Belzoni was also a collector, working for buyers in Europe, and much of what he moved left Egypt. The alabaster sarcophagus he carried out of Seti's tomb is in a museum in London, not in the Valley. That is not how anyone works now, and it should not be romanticized. But the places he opened are still there, they are some of the greatest things a person can see, and the trip that follows him up the Nile is the classic Egypt itinerary done in the right order.

This winter it needs more planning than usual. Two of the great Nile hotels are being rebuilt, the river has a new luxury boat, and the timing of Abu Simbel matters more than most people expect. Here is how I would plan it, and where I would put you.

## When should you go to Egypt for Abu Simbel and the Nile?

October to April. Daytime temperatures in the south sit around 20 to 30°C in those months, while summer in Aswan can pass 40°C, which turns a temple morning into an endurance test. If I had a free choice, I would go in November or in late February and March, with the cool mornings and the long, clear afternoons.

If you can, build the trip around the sun. Twice a year, around February 22 and October 22, the rising sun reaches deep into the Great Temple at Abu Simbel and lights the statues of Ramesses II and the gods in the innermost sanctuary. This October 22 it lit the sanctuary for about twenty minutes, from 6:55 a.m. Hundreds come for it, and some sleep outside the gates. The days either side give you a version of the same effect with a fraction of the crowd, and the 21st and 23rd come closest. February 2027 is the next chance, and the boats and hotels around those dates fill first.

## How do you get to Abu Simbel?

Fly. Abu Simbel is about 280 kilometres south of Aswan, near the Sudanese border: three and a half to four hours each way by road, with day tours leaving Aswan around 4 a.m., or about 45 minutes by air. I would take the flight, arrive for the opening, and see the Great Temple and the smaller Temple of Nefertari before the mid-morning coaches arrive.

Know before you go that the temples you see are not quite where Belzoni found them. Between 1964 and 1968, as the Aswan High Dam was about to flood the valley, an international rescue campaign cut both temples into more than a thousand blocks, each of 20 to 30 tonnes, and rebuilt them on higher ground above the new Lake Nasser. It is one of the great engineering feats of the twentieth century, and once you know it, the seamless cliff face is almost as remarkable as the statues.

## Can you still see the tomb Belzoni found?

Yes, and you should. The tomb of Seti I, KV17 in the Valley of the Kings, is still called "Belzoni's tomb," and it is one of the deepest and most vividly painted in the valley. It is not on the general ticket, which covers three tombs of your choice. It needs a separate ticket of its own, which costs considerably more than the general one, and it has closed for conservation in the past, so have your guide confirm it is open the week you travel. It is worth every pound. Go early, when the valley opens, before the heat and the queues.

If the sarcophagus is the part of the story that stays with you, it is in London. The British Museum turned it down as too expensive, and in 1824 the architect Sir John Soane bought it for £2,000 and threw a three-day party by lamplight to show it off. It still sits in the basement of Sir John Soane's Museum in Lincoln's Inn Fields.

## River or hotel: how should you split the trip?

The classic shape is Cairo, then a flight to Luxor or Aswan, then the Nile between them by boat, with Abu Simbel added from Aswan. Aswan and Luxor are about 240 kilometres apart on the river, and the luxury boats cover it in three or four nights, stopping at Edfu and Kom Ombo on the way. My rule: for a first trip of ten nights or more, do two nights in Cairo, four on the river, two in Aswan with Abu Simbel, and the rest wherever you were happiest. Under eight nights, drop the Aswan hotel and do Abu Simbel as a morning flight from the boat.

The river is the part to book first. The best boats are small, they sail on fixed days, and their good suites are gone months ahead for the winter.

## Where should you stay in Aswan?

### The Old Cataract, under new management

**The Old Cataract** is the hotel Thomas Cook built at the first cataract of the Nile in 1899, and it has been the grand address in Aswan since it opened in January 1900. It is the hotel that appears in the 1978 film of *Death on the Nile*, and Agatha Christie is said to have written part of the novel on its terrace. The story this winter is that Mandarin Oriental took over the management in May 2026 and the hotel is open while it is rebuilt. Only the historic Palace building is taking guests, with 35 rooms and suites, nine rooms and 26 suites, while the newer Nile Wing is fully renovated. The terrace still looks over the river and Elephantine Island; it is the place for afternoon tea at sunset, and Saraya serves Levantine and Mediterranean food at night. There is an infinity pool. Ask for one of the Signature Suites with a private Nile-view terrace. Two honest notes: Forbes lists it as a new opening rather than with a star rating, and Mandarin Oriental's loyalty benefits do not apply until the work is done in 2027. If you want to stay in the hotel rather than the construction site, the Palace building is the hotel, and it is still the best place to sleep the night before Abu Simbel.

## Where should you stay in Luxor?

### Not the Winter Palace, this year

The Winter Palace, Luxor's grand hotel on the corniche, closed in early 2026 for a full restoration and is due to reopen as a Mandarin Oriental in 2027. Plenty of older guides and booking sites still list it. Do not plan around it this season.

### Al Moudira

**Al Moudira** is where I would put you instead, and it may be the better choice anyway. It holds two MICHELIN Keys, which MICHELIN calls "an exceptional stay," and it sits among farmland on the west bank, the quieter side of the river and the side Belzoni worked: the Valley of the Kings, the Ramesseum where he moved the Younger Memnon, and the Colossi of Memnon are all on your doorstep, while the crowds stay on the east bank. It is about 20 minutes from Luxor airport. It looks old but is a new build in the traditional style, with about fifty large, ornate rooms full of stone, tile, gold leaf and antiques, and hammam-style bathrooms. Dinner is in the Great Room, Middle Eastern and European, and there are two bars, the Colonial and the Eastern, plus a pool bar by day, a swimming pool and a Turkish bath. Because it is secluded, you eat most meals there, which is a pleasure rather than a constraint. For two or three nights in Luxor, with early starts in the valley, nothing on the east bank comes close.

## And in Cairo?

**Four Seasons Hotel Cairo at Nile Plaza** is the safe, polished first night, rated Four-Star by Forbes Travel Guide for 2026. It is on the Corniche in Garden City, about five minutes from downtown and close to the Egyptian Museum, with rooms from 495 square feet, most with balconies. Not every room faces the river, so ask for a Premier Nile Room or a suite. Zitouni serves Egyptian food around the clock with Nile views, and Bullona is the first outpost of the Milan restaurant. From here, the second pyramid at Giza, the one Belzoni found his way into, is an easy morning out.

## What Belzoni left

Belzoni never settled. In 1823 he set out to find Timbuktu and died of dysentery that December in what is now Nigeria, at 45. Sarah outlived him by decades and published the portrait of him that hangs in museums now. What is left in Egypt is better than any portrait: a temple he dug out of the sand, the painted tomb of a pharaoh he found in the hills behind Thebes, a pyramid passage he found where no one else had looked. You do not need to be six foot seven to see them. You need the right month, an early flight, and a boat that gets you into the Valley before the heat. We can arrange all three.
