In October 1899, a 31-year-old physics professor from Christiania climbed a mountain above the Altafjord with four assistants and a great deal of equipment, and prepared to spend the winter there. The mountain was Haldde, just over 900 metres high, above the old copper-mining settlement of Kåfjord. The professor was Kristian Birkeland, and he had come to answer a question people in the Arctic had been asking for as long as there had been people in the Arctic: what are the northern lights?

He already had a theory. In 1896 he had proposed that the aurora was caused by streams of charged particles thrown out by the sun and steered by Earth's magnetic field down into the atmosphere near the poles. It was a radical idea, and he needed measurements to prove it. So he built a stone hut on a summit where, according to the University of Oslo's history of the expedition, a storm blew in roughly every other day, the instruments were wrecked, and smoke from the coal stove was forced back down the chimney into the room.

He was right, as it turned out. It took the rest of the century to prove it, and he did not live to see it.

The honest answer on Alta is that it is one of the best places in Europe to see the aurora, that most people treat it as a one-night stop, and that it rewards the traveler who gives it four or five nights and knows the story written on the mountain behind it. Here is what happened on Haldde, how I would see Alta's sky now, and where I would put you.

## Who was Kristian Birkeland?

A Norwegian physicist with too many ideas and very little patience for the people who doubted them. He was born in Christiania, now Oslo, on December 13, 1867, and by his thirties he was working on cathode rays, magnetism and the aurora at the university there. He went north for the first time in 1897, and the Alta Museum records that he nearly died in a gale at minus 25 degrees on the road inland.

He was not only an aurora man. In 1903 he staged a public demonstration in Christiania of an electromagnetic cannon he hoped to sell. A coil short-circuited, and instead of a clean shot the hall got a roaring arc of flame. Birkeland, who had met the industrialist Sam Eyde shortly before, saw what the arc could do: it was hot enough to split nitrogen out of the air. The two men turned it into the Birkeland-Eyde process for making fertilizer, and from it came Norsk Hydro, one of Norway's great industrial companies. He was nominated for the Nobel Prize repeatedly and never won. Norway put his face on the 200-kroner note in 1994, beside a drawing of his terrella, the magnetized sphere in a vacuum box he used to make artificial northern lights in his laboratory.

He died in Tokyo on June 15, 1917, aged 49, on a long, roundabout journey home during the First World War.

## What happened on Haldde?

A brutal winter, a death, and a result that mattered more than anyone knew. Birkeland had asked the government in 1898 for money for two observatories, one on Haldde and one on Talvik, a few kilometres away, and the stations went up in 1899. The plan was to photograph the aurora from both summits at once and work out its height by triangulation.

That part failed. The weather was too violent and the photographic plates were not sensitive enough. Worse, in March 1900, an avalanche on the climb to Haldde killed a young member of the team, Elias Boye. But the magnetometers worked. They recorded the magnetic signature of enormous electric currents flowing in the sky above the polar region, and Birkeland's report of 1901 described them. That was the heart of his theory, on paper, from a stone hut above Kåfjord.

He came back bigger. For the winter of 1902 and 1903, his Norwegian Aurora Polaris Expedition ran four stations across the Arctic, from Spitsbergen to Iceland to Novaya Zemlya, with its centre at Kåfjord. The results filled two large volumes in English, published in 1908.

## Why did it take so long to believe him?

Because the leading British physicists said it could not work. The idea that currents could flow down from space along magnetic field lines was dismissed for decades, most prominently by the geophysicist Sydney Chapman, who argued instead for currents confined to the upper atmosphere. Birkeland's work was treated as a Norwegian curiosity.

Then came satellites. In the late 1960s, magnetometers flown in orbit found exactly the field-aligned currents Birkeland had described from his mountain. They are called Birkeland currents today, and every aurora forecast you check on your phone in Alta rests, in part, on his idea.

## What is left on the mountain?

More than you would expect, and you can sleep in it. After the winter of 1899, Haldde became a permanent observatory: the state funded it in 1910, and in 1912 the physicist Ole Andreas Krogness moved up with his family to run it. Olaf Devik joined in 1915. At its busiest, 17 people lived on the summit, seven of them children, three of whom were born there. Norway's aurora research moved to Tromsø, and Parliament closed Haldde in 1926. In the autumn of 1944, German occupation forces burned the buildings, and only the stone walls were left.

Restoration began in the 1980s. Today the old observatory is a self-service cabin of the Norwegian Trekking Association, DNT, open all year, with six beds, a wood stove, no electricity beyond 12-volt lights and no water on the summit. A night costs NOK 250 for DNT members and NOK 500 for everyone else, and someone in your party needs to be a member to get the standard DNT key. In 2018 the European Physical Society made Haldde its first historic site in Norway, and there is a plaque to prove it.

The summer route is the researchers' old cart road from the E6 near Kåfjord church, roughly eight to nine kilometres and about three hours up at a steady pace. In winter it becomes a ski or crampon climb with real avalanche risk, and DNT asks hikers to check the national avalanche warning service before setting out. My rule: summer on your own, winter only with a guide.

## When should you go to Alta for the northern lights?

Between late August and early April, and the choice inside that window is about what else you want. Visit Norway gives that span as the aurora season. September and October bring dark nights without deep cold, and the chance of seeing the lights over open water. Late December to March is the classic Arctic winter: snow, dog sledding, the igloo hotel and temperatures that average around minus 8 degrees in January and February.

Alta's advantage is its weather. It gets relatively little precipitation for northern Norway, and goes properly dark a short drive out of town. Clear skies matter more than anything else, which is why I would give it four nights at least. The aurora does not keep appointments.

While you are in town, see the Northern Lights Cathedral, the titanium-clad spiral church by the Danish firm Schmidt Hammer Lassen and the Norwegian Link Arkitektur, consecrated in February 2013. And know that Birkeland was not the first scientist drawn to this fjord: the astronomer Martin Brendel photographed the aurora at Bossekop, in what is now Alta, in January 1892.

## Where should you stay?

Split it between dogs and ice. **Holmen Husky Lodge**, a family-run dog-sledding outfit with roots in the mid-1980s just outside Alta, has freestanding timber Forest Suites set among the pines, with floor-to-ceiling glass so you can watch for the aurora from bed when the sky allows. Dinners are communal, in a restored barn, there is a shared sauna, and the Alaskan huskies are the point. Two nights here, with a day on the sled, is where I would start.

Then one night, not more, at the **Sorrisniva Igloo Hotel**, on the Alta River about 11 kilometres from the town centre. It is rebuilt every winter by artists and builders around a new theme, from about 250 tonnes of ice and 7,000 cubic metres of snow, and for 2026 to 2027 it takes overnight guests from December 20 to March 31. Book an Igloo Suite, the themed rooms, and sleep in a warm sleeping bag on a bed of ice. Rates start at about NOK 4,070 for a basic igloo room. For the nights either side, Sorrisniva's year-round **Arctic Wilderness Lodge** next door has 24 rooms and suites with floor-to-ceiling windows on the river and the Maku restaurant.

Neither is a grand hotel, and I would not pretend otherwise. They are the right places for this trip, and rooms go early for the darkest weeks, so let our advisors quote current rates and hold the dates.

## The mountain, in the end

Birkeland spent a winter on Haldde trying to photograph the northern lights and failed. What he caught instead, almost by accident, was the invisible part: the currents that make the lights. It took sixty years and a satellite for the world to agree. Stand outside at Holmen on a clear night in February, watch the sky over the Altafjord turn green, and remember the stone hut on the mountain above Kåfjord. He worked it out up there.
