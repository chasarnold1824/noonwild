In 1929 a Berlin doctor named Friedrich Ritter and his companion, Dore Strauch, left their spouses in Germany and sailed for Floreana, a dry, empty island at the southern edge of the Galápagos. They meant to live there alone, on what they could grow, as far from Europe as two people could get. It did not stay that way for long. Strauch's letters home found their way into the newspapers, Ritter wrote a series of articles for The Atlantic, and the island at the end of the world became, briefly, the most talked-about address in the Pacific.

In 1932 a German family arrived: Heinz Wittmer, his pregnant wife Margret and his teenage son Harry. Later that year came the woman who would make Floreana famous, an Austrian who called herself the Baroness, with two younger men, Robert Philippson and Rudolf Lorenz. She styled herself the Empress of Floreana and announced plans for a hotel for wealthy yachtsmen. On March 27, 1934, she and Philippson disappeared. The Wittmers and Lorenz said the pair had left for Tahiti on a friend's yacht. Nobody else saw a yacht in the bay, and the Baroness left her prized possessions behind.

Lorenz left soon after with a Norwegian fisherman. Months later, two mummified bodies were found on Marchena, a waterless island far to the north; the men appear to have died of thirst. Before the year was out, Ritter was dead too, of food poisoning from spoiled chicken. Strauch went home to Germany. Margret Wittmer stayed, lived on Floreana until her death in 2000, and never told anyone anything that settled the matter. Ron Howard turned the story into a film, Eden, which premiered in 2024 and reached cinemas in 2025.

The honest answer for travelers is that you do not go to Floreana for the mystery. You go because it is one of the best days of any Galápagos voyage: a whalers' post office that still works, a lagoon of flamingos, some of the best snorkeling in the islands and, since this February, giant tortoises back on the island after more than 150 years. But Floreana is a day, not a base, and which ship you choose decides how good that day is. Here is how I would plan it, and where I would put you.

## Can you visit Floreana, and how?

Yes, and almost always by ship. Floreana is inhabited, but its only village, Puerto Velasco Ibarra, is home to about 160 people, according to the Galapagos Conservation Trust, and the places to stay are simple. The way to see it well is a full day on a voyage that includes it, with the ship anchored off the island overnight.

Not every Galápagos itinerary goes south. Ships rotate between routes, and Floreana usually sits on the western or southern loops. Before you book, read the day-by-day, and look for three things on the Floreana day: Post Office Bay, Punta Cormorant and a snorkel at either Champion Islet or Devil's Crown. If a route gives you only a quick landing at Post Office Bay, it is not the one.

## What do you actually see on Floreana?

<figure><img src="https://cdn.expeditions.com/globalassets/der-images/it/it162454/it091524-a.jpg?width=1920&height=1080&mode=crop&scale=none&quality=50" alt="Guests ashore on Floreana in the Galápagos during a National Geographic Islander II voyage." loading="lazy"><figcaption>A day on Floreana with National Geographic Islander II, September 2024. Image courtesy of <a href="https://expeditions.com/expedition-stories/daily-expedition-reports/floreana-island-IT-2024-09-15/galapagos" rel="noopener">Lindblad Expeditions</a>.</figcaption></figure>

**Post Office Bay** comes first, usually in the morning. Whalers in the Pacific were leaving letters here more than two centuries ago, in a box on a post, for any ship heading home to carry onward. A barrel has stood in its place for well over a hundred years, and the custom still works: you leave a postcard, unstamped, and look through the pile for any addressed near your own home, which you then deliver by hand. It sounds like a gimmick. It is one of the few rituals in the islands that has run, without interruption, since the age of sail.

**Punta Cormorant** is the afternoon landing on the north shore. A short trail runs from an olive-green beach to a brackish lagoon where American flamingos feed, sometimes dozens at a time, and on to a white-sand beach where rays rest in the shallows and Sally Lightfoot crabs work the rocks. Bring a long lens. The flamingos are shy.

**Devil's Crown and Champion Islet** are the snorkels. Devil's Crown is the eroded rim of a volcanic cone just offshore, where reef fish school inside the crater and hammerheads or white-tipped reef sharks may pass. Champion, a small islet nearby, is one of only two places where the Floreana mockingbird survives; the bird is gone from the main island. You snorkel around it rather than walk it.

**The highlands** are the part most voyages skip. This is where the settlers made their homes, in the green hills above the dry coast. The closest most voyages come to the Baroness is her lookout over the coast, a stop on Aqua Mare's western route. If the history is the reason you are going, ask your advisor for a ship that goes inland.

## The tortoises are back

On February 20, 2026, the Galápagos National Park and its partners released 158 young giant tortoises on Floreana. The island's own tortoise, *Chelonoidis niger niger*, was driven to extinction in the mid-1800s, most likely by whalers, who took tortoises aboard as fresh meat for long voyages. But whalers also moved tortoises between islands, and in the early 2000s geneticists found tortoises on Wolf Volcano, at the far north of Isabela, carrying Floreana ancestry. A breeding programme on Santa Cruz brought the line as close as it could to the original.

It is the first step in a much larger project. Rats and feral cats are being removed from the island, and the plan is to bring back twelve locally extinct species in all, including the Floreana mockingbird and the Floreana racer, a small snake. For a traveler, it means that Floreana, for decades the island where things vanished, is now the island where they are coming back. Your guide will know where the tortoises are.

## When should you go?

The Galápagos works year-round, but the two seasons are different trips.

**December to May** is the warm season: warmer water, calmer seas, better visibility for snorkeling, and the sunniest weather from January onward, with short showers. Sea turtles nest early in the year, and sea lion pups gather on the beaches in spring. If anyone in your party is nervous about seasickness, this is the window.

**June to November** is the cool season, when the Humboldt Current brings colder, richer water up from the south. Marine life is at its most abundant, seabirds are busy, and blue-footed boobies are courting and then raising chicks. The price is choppier water: August and September are the coolest and roughest months, easing by October and November. On a small yacht, that matters more than on a big ship.

If I had a free choice, I would go in April or May, or in November, with the year's best weather on one side and its best wildlife on the other. Book nine to twelve months ahead for the holidays; the best ships are small, and the best suites go first.

## Which ship should you book?

These are the three I would look at first for a Floreana voyage, and the order depends on who is travelling.

### The classic: Silver Origin

<figure><img src="https://secure.s.forbestravelguide.com/img/properties/silver-origin/silver-origin-owners-suite.jpg" alt="The Owner's Suite aboard Silver Origin, Silversea's Galápagos ship, with floor-to-ceiling windows over the sea." loading="lazy"><figcaption>The Owner's Suite on Silver Origin. Image courtesy of <a href="https://forbestravelguide.com/ocean-cruises/silver-origin" rel="noopener">Silversea via Forbes Travel Guide</a>.</figcaption></figure>

**Silver Origin** is Silversea's ship built for the Galápagos, and it is the only Forbes Four-Star ship in the islands for 2026. It carries 100 guests, all in suites, every one with a balcony and a butler, which no other ship here offers. It arrived in 2020 to replace Silver Galapagos, and it was designed for these waters: it holds position over fragile reefs without dropping anchor, and every suite has its own filtered water so the ship can run without plastic bottles. The interiors are calm and modern, with floor-to-ceiling glass and Ecuadorian art throughout, and some suites have bathtubs with a sea view. Dinner is in The Restaurant or at The Grill, open-air, where the signature at dinner is Silversea's Hot Rocks, steak and seafood cooked on hot volcanic stone. Briefings happen in the Explorer Lounge, and there is one naturalist for every ten guests, among the best ratios in the islands. On the current seven-night loop from San Cristóbal, the Floreana day is a full one: Post Office Bay, Champion Islet and Punta Cormorant. Book Silver Origin for a couple, or for travelers who want hotel-level service and a bigger, steadier ship in the rough months. Let our advisors quote current fares; they move with the season and the suite.

### The private choice: Aqua Mare

**Aqua Mare** is the opposite answer: a 164-foot superyacht, built by CRN in Italy, with interiors by François Zuretti, seven suites and no more than sixteen guests. Aqua Expeditions launched it in the Galápagos in 2022. The suites run from compact cabins to an owner's suite of about 860 square feet, and that is the one to ask for if you are taking the whole yacht. There are sixteen crew, two of them naturalist guides, so service runs close to one to one. The menu is by Pedro Miguel Schiaffino, the Peruvian chef, in a Nikkei style built on local fish. On deck there is a six-person whirlpool and a barbecue; at the stern, a beach club with a hydraulic window that opens to the sea, plus kayaks, paddleboards and two black tenders that take no more than eight guests ashore at a time. Drinks, excursions and park fees are included. The western route spends its second day on Floreana, with Punta Cormorant, Post Office Bay and the Baroness's lookout. This is the one I would charter for a family or a group of friends: you fill it, you set the pace, and nobody else is at the barrel when you get there.

### The naturalist's ship: National Geographic Islander II

**National Geographic Islander II** is Lindblad Expeditions' 48-guest ship, the former Crystal Esprit, rebuilt with 26 suites and sailing in the Galápagos since August 2022. It is less of a luxury product than the other two, and more of an expedition: kayaks and paddleboards, a custom glass-bottom Zodiac for guests who would rather not snorkel, and a science hub on board. Its Floreana days are among the fullest, with Post Office Bay, a kayak or snorkel in the bay, Champion Islet and Punta Cormorant. Book Islander II for curious families with older children, and for anyone who would rather spend dinner talking to a marine biologist than ordering wine.

## Where to stay on land

Most people should not try to sleep on Floreana. But two properties are worth knowing.

<figure><img src="https://d1m7xnn75ypr6t.cloudfront.net/transform/eeab5829-6350-4d6c-bdca-70768ed460b4/?io=transform:fill,width:3840&format=webp&quality=80" alt="Pikaia Lodge on the rim of an extinct volcanic crater in the highlands of Santa Cruz, Galápagos." loading="lazy"><figcaption>Pikaia Lodge, Santa Cruz. Image courtesy of <a href="https://relaischateaux.com/us/hotel/pikaia-lodge" rel="noopener">Pikaia Lodge via Relais & Châteaux</a>.</figcaption></figure>

**Pikaia Lodge**, on Santa Cruz, is the Galápagos for travelers who do not want to sleep at sea. It is a Relais & Châteaux member with 14 rooms on the edge of an extinct volcanic crater, on 31 hectares with its own giant tortoise reserve, an infinity pool, a spa and a fine-dining restaurant. The rooms are 61 square metres with floor-to-ceiling glass; the ones to ask for are the Pool Suites, 74 square metres with their own plunge pool. Days are spent on the lodge's yacht, M/Y Vision Pikaia, which the lodge calls the only private hotel yacht in the Galápagos, with six guest cabins. Rates are all-inclusive, from around $3,900 a night. The first phase of a renovation debuts this autumn, and the lodge reopened for the season on November 9. Book it for a land-based week, or as the first three nights before a voyage, and ask your advisor to build a Floreana day into the yacht programme.

**Floreana Lava Lodge**, on the island itself, is the honest alternative: ten cabins built of mainland pine, each with a porch and a sea view, a few minutes from the village and the sea lions on its beach. It is simple, sold only as part of a land package, and not a luxury stay. It is for the traveler who wants to be on Floreana after the ships have gone, which is when the island feels most like the place the settlers came to find.

## What the barrel still knows

Nobody solved the Floreana mystery, and nobody is likely to now. What lasted were the things the settlers came for: the dry hills, the green highlands, the bay where whalers left their letters. The Wittmers' descendants still keep a hotel and a post office in the village. The barrel still takes postcards. And the tortoises, gone since before any of the settlers were born, are back on the slopes above the bay. Leave a card for someone at home. Take one to deliver. It is the oldest custom in the islands, and the only one that never vanished.
