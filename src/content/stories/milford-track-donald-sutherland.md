On the 3rd of December 1877, a small open boat came round the point into Milford Sound and a man in his thirties stepped ashore with his dog. He had sailed it, by himself, from Dunedin. His name was Donald Sutherland, he had been a fisherman, a militiaman, a volunteer for Garibaldi, a gold prospector and a soldier, and he had decided that this was where he would stop. He stayed, more or less, for the rest of his life.

The first thing he did was plan a city. He drew up streets for a settlement he called the City of Milford, and for years its population was one. The second thing he did, three years later, was walk inland looking for a way through the mountains, see water flashing through the trees, and find the waterfall that now carries his name. He told everyone it was the highest in the world. It was not, but it was close enough to bring the tourists, and the tourists are why there is a Milford Track at all.

The honest answer on the Milford Track is that it deserves its reputation, it fills up faster than almost anything else in New Zealand, and the version of it that most people book skips the part of the story I like best, which happens after the walk ends. Here is Sutherland's story, and that of the man who found the pass he never could, and how I would walk it this season.

## Who was Donald Sutherland?

A Scot from Wick, in Caithness, born in 1843 or 1844, the son of a ropemaker, who seems to have tried every job on offer to a restless young man in the 1860s. He left the herring fishery for a militia unit at Fort George at about sixteen. In 1860 he joined the British volunteers who fought with Garibaldi in Italy, following the campaign through Sicily to Naples. Then he sailed to Dunedin, deserted his ship, and went looking for gold at Gabriels Gully.

Gold did not work out. He enlisted in the Waikato Militia in 1863 and the Armed Constabulary in 1868, served through the New Zealand Wars, and came out a corporal with a campaign medal. By 1877 he was ready, as he is said to have put it, to come to anchor. He chose the most remote harbour in the country to do it in.

He spent the next years as the Milford Sound hermit, prospecting the fiord's walls for asbestos and bowenite with two companions, John McKay and James Malcolm, without much success. NZHistory sums up his career with a frankness I appreciate: he was unsuccessful as a prospector and unfulfilled as an explorer. That makes what happened next more likely to have mattered to him.

## What did he find in 1880?

On the 10th of November 1880, near the Arthur River, Sutherland and McKay were working their way up the valley looking for a route from the sound towards Lake Wakatipu. Through the trees Sutherland caught a glimpse of falling water, a long way up. It was the outflow of Lake Quill, dropping into the Arthur Valley in three great leaps, and he was the first European to see it.

He was not modest about it. Sutherland claimed the falls were well over 1,000 metres, which would have made them the highest in the world by a distance, and for a while New Zealanders repeated the claim with pride. Later surveys brought the figure down to about 580 metres, in three drops of roughly 229, 248 and 103 metres. That still makes them the tallest falls in New Zealand, and standing at the base, soaked by the spray, you will not feel shortchanged.

The two men shared the credit in the most practical way available. The story told on the track is that they tossed a coin for the naming rights to the falls they found. Sutherland won the big one. McKay got the smaller cascade you pass further down the valley, which is still called Mackay Falls.

## Who actually found the Milford Track?

Not Sutherland, which is the irony at the centre of this story. He spent years looking for an overland route between Milford Sound and the lakes to the east and never found one. In 1888 the government paid him to cut a track from the Arthur Valley up to his falls, which became the last stretch of the walk. The pass that connects it all was found the same year by another Scot.

Quintin McKinnon was from Argyllshire, had come to New Zealand in the 1870s, and was hired by the Otago Survey Department to cut a track up the Clinton Valley from Lake Te Anau and look for a way over the top. On the 17th of October 1888, after a month of hacking through forest in foul weather with his partner Ernest Mitchell, he crossed the saddle now called Omanui / McKinnon Pass. According to the Department of Conservation, three days after they pushed on ahead, they came down onto the track Sutherland had already cut on the other side.

For the next few years McKinnon was the route's first guide, improving the track, leading parties of walkers from Lake Te Anau to Sutherland Falls and carrying the mail between Te Anau and Milford. On the 29th of November 1892 he set out across Lake Te Anau towards the start of the track and never arrived. A search found his wrecked boat and his belongings, but never his body. In 1914 the Gaelic Society of New Zealand finished paying for a stone cairn on the pass, and it is still there, at the highest point of the walk. The Quintin huts and Quintin Lodge, near the turn-off to the falls, are named for him.

## What happened to the City of Milford?

It became a hotel, which is the part of the story I like best. In 1890 Sutherland went back to Dunedin and married Elizabeth Samuels, a widow from Bedford in England, and she bought the land. Together they built the Chalet, an accommodation house at the sound for walkers coming off the new track. Te Ara records 40 visitors to the falls in the 1888-89 season and 70 the year after: small numbers, but the beginning of everything Milford is now.

The track's reputation was made, oddly, in London. In March 1908 the poet Blanche Baughan walked it, and according to the *Listener* signed the Sutherlands' visitors' book at the Chalet on the 21st. She sent her account to the *Spectator*, whose editor cut it down hard and published it on the 12th of September 1908 under a title that New Zealand has used ever since: "The Finest Walk in the World."

Donald Sutherland died at Milford on the 24th of October 1919. Elizabeth was alone with him, and could not move or bury him herself; his body lay for five weeks until the next government steamer arrived. She stayed on, sold the Chalet to the government in 1922 for £1,000, and died at Milford the following year. They are buried together at the sound.

## When should you go, and how do you book it?

Between November and April, and book the moment bookings open. The Great Walks season for 2026-27 runs from 1 November 2026 to 30 April 2027, with the last departure on 28 April. In season the track runs one way only, from Glade Wharf at the head of Lake Te Anau to Sandfly Point near Milford, over four days and three nights, sleeping at Clinton, Mintaro and Dumpling huts. Huts must be booked in advance, at NZ$152 per international adult per night.

Hut bookings for the whole year open on a single day in May, through a virtual queue, and the peak of summer goes quickly: Milford huts ran at about 95 per cent occupancy at the height of last season. If you have missed this summer's window, plan for next November now. Outside the season bookings are not needed and bunks are first come, first served, but that means a winter crossing of an alpine pass, and I would not plan the trip around it.

Transport is part of the booking. There is no road to either end, so you take a bus to Te Anau Downs, about 30 minutes from Te Anau, then a boat of about an hour and a quarter to Glade Wharf, and at the far end a 20-minute boat from Sandfly Point to Milford Sound.

## Should you walk it guided or independently?

Guided, unless the bunk room and the camp stove are part of the point for you. Independent walkers sleep in DOC huts with bunks and cook their own food. The guided walk, run by Ultimate Hikes, uses its own private lodges along the same track: five days and four nights, with Glade House on the first night, Pompolona Lodge on the second and Quintin Lodge on the third, then a final night at Mitre Peak Lodge at Milford Sound. You carry a day pack, sleep in a bed, shower hot, dry your gear in a drying room and eat a three-course dinner. Private rooms with their own bathroom are available, and they are the ones to ask for. On the operator's current price list, private twin rooms start at NZ$3,750 per person, with shared rooms from NZ$2,925, including transport from Queenstown or Te Anau, all meals and the cruise on the sound at the end.

Whichever way you walk, do the side trip. From the turn-off near Quintin Lodge it is about an hour and a half there and back to the base of Sutherland Falls, over two swing bridges. Go even if it is raining. The falls are better in rain, and in Fiordland, which gets around 6,300 millimetres a year, the odds are good that it will be.

## Where should you stay at Milford Sound?

At the sound itself, for at least one night after the walk, because that is where Sutherland's story ends and where the day-trippers leave you in peace. The guided walk ends at **Mitre Peak Lodge**, which Ultimate Hikes describes as the only accommodation right on the edge of the sound, and the view down the fiord to Mitre Peak, which rises about 1,700 metres from the water, is what you are paying for.

Independent walkers, or anyone driving the 120 kilometres in from Te Anau, should book **Milford Sound Lodge** at the head of the Cleddau Valley, which began in 1935 as a road workers' camp and has housed Milford Track walkers since 1950. The six Premium Riverside Chalets, renovated in the winter of 2025, are the ones to ask for: cedar-lined, with a king bed, floor-to-ceiling windows on the Cleddau River and a private outdoor bath, and breakfast from the Pio Pio Restaurant delivered to the door. They are adults only and take a two-night minimum from October to April, so they suit the walker who wants a day of rest before the drive out. Let our advisors quote current rates and hold the chalet for the night you come off the track.

## Where to find him

Milford Sound has a Māori name older than any of this, Piopiotahi, a single thrush, from the story of the bird that flew south after the death of Māui. Sutherland was a late arrival, and a short one in the life of the place. But on your last morning, walk behind the café near the carpark, past the two brown buildings, to the start of the Milford Sound Lookout Track. Donald and Elizabeth Sutherland are buried there, at the beginning of a 20-minute path to a view of the sound. He planned a city here and got a guesthouse, looked for a pass and found a waterfall, and the walk that grew out of his mistakes is the one people still cross the world to do. I cannot think of a better place for him.
